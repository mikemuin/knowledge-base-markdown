# The 1-3-6 Framework: A Strategic Discourse

## What You've Actually Built

Before we refine anything, let's name what this is precisely: **a temporal impact lattice**. Most planning frameworks fail because they either collapse everything into a single horizon (the 90-day plan that ignores today's fires) or they stack horizons without defining how they *interact*. The 1-3-6 Framework is valuable because it implicitly acknowledges that decisions have **different decay rates** — some choices are fully resolved in a day, others won't reveal their true cost or benefit for six months.

That's not just a planning tool. That's a diagnostic instrument.

------

## The Three Horizons and What They Actually Measure

The numbers matter less than what each horizon *represents psychologically and operationally*.

**The 1 (One Day)** is your ground truth horizon. It captures the immediate, tactile reality of a decision — what happens on the floor, in the inbox, in the room, *today*. When you're assessing an IT system or a leadership decision through this lens, you're asking: what friction or relief does a human being experience *right now*? This is where adoption lives or dies. A system that scores poorly at Day 1 almost never recovers, because first impressions in operational environments calcify into cultural resistance. The 1-horizon is ruthlessly honest precisely because it strips away narrative.

**The 3 (Three Weeks)** is the behavioral integration horizon. Three weeks is long enough that novelty has worn off, short enough that formal processes haven't yet adapted to mask problems. This is where you see whether change is *sticking* or whether people have found workarounds. In project management, this is your early warning system — the canary in the coal mine for scope creep, adoption failure, and misaligned incentives. In IT system assessment, this is when ticket volume, error rates, and informal workarounds surface. In leadership decisions, this is when you see whether communication landed or whether the org has quietly reverted to its prior operating model.

**The 6 (Six Months)** is the structural consequence horizon. This is where second-order effects appear — the impacts that weren't in the original model. Budget reallocation, team attrition, technical debt, culture drift, and competitive positioning shifts. A decision that looked clean at Day 1 and functional at Week 3 can still be quietly destroying organizational capacity at Month 6. This horizon demands different data: not operational metrics but structural ones. Retention rates, system reliability trends, project velocity, and strategic alignment.

------

## Why This Geometry Works

The 1-3-6 spacing is not arbitrary, and I'd argue you should defend these intervals deliberately rather than treat them as placeholder numbers.

The ratio between horizons is roughly **1:21:180** in days. Each jump is approximately an order of magnitude larger. This mirrors how **consequence complexity compounds** — decisions don't just add impact linearly over time, they *branch*. One day has limited branching. Three weeks have moderate branching. Six months have exponential branching. Your framework intuitively encodes this without mathematizing it, which makes it accessible without being naive.

Compare this to the OKR quarterly cadence, which collapses everything into a 90-day window and treats all impact as equally visible within that period. Or to traditional waterfall project phases, which are milestone-bound rather than impact-bound. The 1-3-6 Framework is **impact-bound**, which is a fundamentally different and superior epistemic stance.

------

## The Dual Application: Planning and Assessment

This is where your framework gains genuine strategic depth — the fact that it works in *both directions*.

**As a planning tool**, 1-3-6 forces a discipline most planners avoid: defining *what success looks like at each horizon before you begin*. This is not the same as setting milestones. Milestones are activity-based. 1-3-6 impact definitions are *outcome-based at the human level*. What does a person, team, or system *feel and do differently* at each horizon? This forces the planner to think in behavioral outcomes, not deliverables.

**As an assessment tool**, particularly for IT systems, the framework becomes a structured post-implementation review lens. You're not asking "did we deliver on scope?" You're asking three harder questions: Did it change behavior today? Is that change persisting at three weeks? Is it generating compounding organizational value at six months? These questions expose implementation theater — the projects that ship on time and under budget but fail completely at driving the intended outcome.

The dual application also creates **feedback symmetry**. If you planned with 1-3-6 and you assess with 1-3-6, you have a direct line between your original hypothesis and your empirical results. That's a learning loop most organizations never build.

------

## Refinements Worth Considering

A few structural enhancements to sharpen the framework before you finalize it.

First, consider naming what changes *between* horizons, not just at each horizon. The transition from 1 to 3 is the **Adoption Gap**—the window when most change initiatives collapse. The transition from 3 to 6 is the **Entrenchment Window** — where behavior either becomes systemic or quietly regresses. Naming these transitions gives practitioners a mechanism to *intervene* rather than just *observe*.

Second, the framework would benefit from a **confidence weight** at each horizon. Day 1 assessments are high-confidence but low-signal. Month 6 assessments are high-signal but high-uncertainty because more variables have entered the system. Being explicit about this prevents teams from over-indexing on immediate feedback (the common failure mode) or deferring all judgment to long-term data (the paralysis failure mode).

Third, think about **who owns each horizon**. In most organizations, the 1-day horizon is owned by frontline operators, the 3-week horizon by middle management, and the 6-month horizon by executive leadership. The framework implicitly defines an accountability architecture. Making that explicit turns it into a governance tool, not just a planning tool.

------

## The Strategic Case for Adoption

The reason this framework deserves to be formalized is that most organizations live in a state of **temporal incoherence** — executives talking in years, managers in quarters, and individual contributors in days, with no shared translation layer among them. The 1-3-6 Framework *is* that translation layer. It doesn't eliminate the tension between horizons, but it makes the tension legible, which is a prerequisite for managing it.

The best frameworks are those that change what questions get asked in a room. This one does that. It replaces "are we on track?" with "what is the impact at each horizon, and does that pattern match our original hypothesis?" That is a categorically better question, and the organizations that ask better questions consistently outperform those that don't.

That is the strategic case. The rest is execution.
