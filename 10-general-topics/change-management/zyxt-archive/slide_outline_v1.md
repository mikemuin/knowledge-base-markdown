# Slide Outline — Failure-Aware Change Management Framework

**Target audience:** Non-technical professionals (conference) **Target time:** 20 minutes (~24 slides) **Section structure:** Introduction · How To Use · Technology · Process · People · Synthesis

------

## SECTION 1 — INTRODUCTION

*5 slides | ~4 minutes*

------

### Slide 1 — Title

**Title:** Why Health IT Projects Fail — And What To Do About I

**Subtitle:** The Failure-Aware Change Management Framework

**Notes:** Set tone immediately. Field-tested, not theoretical.

------

### Slide 2 — The Problem

**Title:** The Pattern That Keeps Repeating

**Content:** Three statements, each a common project post-mortem:

- "The system went live on time — but staff never really adopted it."
- "The process looked right on paper — but that's not how anyone actually worked."
- "Leadership was supportive — until they weren't."

**Subtext:** These are not isolated failures. They are the same failure, wearing different clothes.

**Notes:** Open with recognition. Every person in the room has a version of one of these stories.

------

### Slide 3 — The Core Statement

**Title:** One Sentence. One Framework.

**Content:**

> *"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

**Notes:** Two deliberate provocations: failure is the *starting assumption*, and this work runs *alongside* the project — not after delivery.

------

### Slide 4 — The Five Governing Principles

**Title:** Five Governing Principles

**Content:** One line each:

1. **Start on day one, not day done** — change management begins with the project, not after delivery
2. **Engineer for failure, not for optimism** — success is what you build against the default
3. **What you can't see will hurt you most** — silent failure is the most dangerous kind
4. **If everyone owns it, no one owns it** — accountability must be assigned, not assumed
5. **Not all failure costs the same** — a training gap is recoverable; a patient safety event is not

**Notes:** These are operating logic, not motivational statements. Let the honesty of #2 and #3 register.

------

### Slide 5 — The Three Layers

**Title:** Three Layers. One Framework.

**Content:** Visual — three distinct elements with role labels:

- **People** — the Behavior System
- **Process** — the Execution System
- **Technology** — the Enablement System

**Subtext:** Not departments. The three surfaces on which every Health IT implementation succeeds or fails — and the three directions from which failure most often arrives.

**Notes:** Brief. The next three sections go deep on each layer. This is orientation, not explanation.

------

## SECTION 2 — HOW TO USE THE FRAMEWORK

*4 slides | ~3 minutes*

------

### Slide 6 — The Three-Step Loop

**Title:** Identify. Question. Implement.

**Content:**

- **Identify** — take inventory of what belongs to each layer: the people involved, the workflows changing, the systems being deployed
- **Question** — run six structured questions against each item identified
- **Implement** — embed the answers into the live project as a running discipline, not a planning document

**Subtext:** This is not a one-time exercise. It runs from the first planning meeting through go-live and beyond.

**Notes:** The loop framing matters. It signals iteration, not completion.

------

### Slide 7 — The Six Questions

**Title:** The Same Six Questions. Applied to Everything.

**Content:** Two columns of three:

- What does success look like?
- What are we taking for granted?
- Where could we fail?
- How will we know if something is going wrong?
- What do we do now to reduce the chance of failure?
- If something fails, what do we do?

**Notes:** Spend time here. These questions are the intellectual engine of the framework. For a conference audience, name one question that is almost always skipped: "How will we know if something is going wrong?"

------

### Slide 8 — Turning Answers Into Action

**Title:** From Analysis to Live Project Management

**Content:** Five output types, one per row:

- **Success definitions** → milestone checkpoints; the project does not advance on schedule alone
- **Assumptions** → live tracker, reviewed at every major milestone
- **Risks** → risk register, organized by layer and severity, updated continuously
- **Detection methods** → active from day one, transferred to operations at project close
- **Response protocols** → written and staged before they are needed

**Notes:** Emphasize that these are not reports — they are embedded project tools. A risk register no one reviews is a document, not a management instrument.

------

### Slide 9 — The Framework Does Not End at Go-Live

**Title:** The Project Ends. The Framework Doesn't.

**Content:** Two-column contrast:

- **What ends at go-live:** the project structure, the implementation team, the launch milestones
- **What must continue:** detection cadence, assumption tracking, stakeholder monitoring, process compliance checks, system health reviews

**Subtext:** The framework ends when accountability has been formally transferred to operations — and when those people have the tools, the knowledge, and the authority to keep running it.

**Notes:** Plant this here. It will be reinforced in each layer section and echoed in the Synthesis.

------

## SECTION 3 — TECHNOLOGY LAYER

*4 slides | ~3.5 minutes*

------

### Slide 10 — Technology: What We're Managing

**Title:** Technology — The Enablement System

**Content:**

- **What it is:** the most visible layer — the system staff log into every day
- **The trap:** visibility creates overconfidence; you can point to the system, but that does not mean the system is working
- **What success actually requires:** the right system, reliably accessible, easy enough to use, improving the work it was meant to support — and that remains true long after launch

**Notes:** The "you can point to it" framing cuts through the common assumption that a running system is a successful one.

------

### Slide 11 — The Go-Live Trap

**Title:** Launch Day Is Not the Finish Line

**Content:** Timeline visual:

- Launch → [support withdrawn] → [small problems accumulate] → [workarounds normalize] → [quiet deterioration]

**Subtext:** Organisations that declare victory on launch day stop actively managing the technology at precisely the moment when the most significant risks begin to emerge.

**Notes:** This is the most important single point in the Technology section for a non-technical audience. Make it land.

------

### Slide 12 — Six Technology Failure Areas

**Title:** Where Technology Actually Fails

**Content:** Table or icon grid — one row per failure area:

- **T1** · Wrong system selected silent
- **T2** · Access and infrastructure failures
- **T3** · Data accuracy and system connections silent
- **T4** · Staff not using the system silent
- **T5** · System does not improve the work silent
- **T6** · Decline after launch

**Subtext:** Four of six can fail without any obvious warning sign.

**Notes:** The ⚠ markers do the work here. Silent failure is the recurring theme across all three layers.

------

### Slide 13 — Technology: The Cross-Layer Effect

**Title:** Technology Problems Don't Stay in Technology

**Content:** Three triggered effects, each one line:

- Data accuracy failure → staff develop informal workarounds → process becomes inconsistent
- Staff not using the system → work splits between system and paper → no one has a complete picture
- Wrong system selected → staff trust is damaged early → resistance gains a legitimate basis

**Subtext:** Treating technology problems as purely technical produces technical solutions that miss the actual cause.

**Notes:** Bridge into the Process section.

------

## SECTION 4 — PROCESS LAYER

*4 slides | ~3.5 minutes*

------

### Slide 14 — Process: What We're Managing

**Title:** Process — The Execution System

**Content:**

- **What it is:** the transition from how things are done today to how they need to be done tomorrow
- **The hidden complexity:** every clinical environment runs on two versions of every process — the official one, and the real one
- **What success actually requires:** the new workflow executing correctly, by all staff, in real conditions, without regression

**Notes:** The "two versions" framing is the most useful thing to land in this slide. It explains why planning room exercises so consistently miss the point.

------

### Slide 15 — The Transition Period

**Title:** The Most Dangerous Operational State in Any Implementation

**Content:**

- The transition period: when old and new processes run simultaneously
- What happens inside it: some records in the system, some on paper; no one has a complete picture; mistakes happen; information gets lost
- The critical risk: within ~30 days of launch, if both processes are still running, staff stop perceiving the situation as temporary. It becomes the new normal.

**Subtext:** A controlled step backward is recoverable. A normalised transition period is not.

**Notes:** This is the organising risk of the entire Process Layer. Emphasise the 30-day window — it's specific, surprising, and actionable.

------

### Slide 16 — Seven Process Failure Areas

**Title:** Where Process Actually Fails

**Content:** Table — one row per failure area:

- **P1** · Getting the current picture wrong silent
- **P2** · Designing a process that cannot survive reality
- **P3** · Transition period becoming permanent silent
- **P4** · Missing the unofficial workarounds silent
- **P5** · Process working for champions, not for everyone silent
- **P6** · Process works but produces no improvement silent
- **P7** · Sliding back to the old way of working

**Subtext:** Five of seven can fail without any obvious warning sign.

**Notes:** Draw attention to P4 specifically — unofficial workarounds are the most reliably missed item in any planning exercise.

------

### Slide 17 — Process: The Cross-Layer Effect

**Title:** Process Problems Don't Stay in Process

**Content:** Three triggered effects:

- Inaccurate current state picture → system configured against a false foundation → technology misfit at deployment
- Transition period normalises → data splits between old process and new system → reports become unreliable; clinically untrustworthy
- Regression goes unchallenged → champions lose their rationale for advocacy → people layer erodes

**Subtext:** Different problems need different decision-makers. Getting the picture right requires front-line access. Closing the transition requires operational authority. Sustaining the process requires the management structure to own it.

**Notes:** Bridge into the People section.

------

## SECTION 5 — PEOPLE LAYER

*4 slides | ~3.5 minutes*

------

### Slide 18 — People: What We're Managing

**Title:** People — The Behavior System

**Content:**

- **What it is:** stakeholder behavior from start to finish — engagement, ownership, and adoption across the full arc of the project
- **What makes it different:** failure here is neither visible nor observable. It accumulates in silence.
- **What success actually requires:** enough stakeholders of sufficient authority positioned at or above passive support — with active champions distributed at the critical nodes across the organisation

**Notes:** The contrast with Technology and Process ("Technology failure is visible. Process failure is observable. People failure is neither.") is the most important framing in this section.

------

### Slide 19 — The Neutral Zone

**Title:** The Largest Addressable Population Is Waiting

**Content:**

- Most stakeholders are neither resisting nor supporting — they are watching
- What they are watching for: momentum, the behavior of people they respect, organizational signals
- How they are moved: not by communication plans or training programs — by peer influence from someone who has already decided

**Subtext:** Moving even a portion of the neutral zone toward passive support has more impact on project momentum than converting a single active resistor.

**Notes:** This reframes the entire communication and engagement strategy. The implication: champion placement matters more than champion headcount.

------

### Slide 20 — Seven People Failure Areas

**Title:** Where People Actually Fails

**Content:** Table — one row per failure area:

- **Pe1** · Stakeholder mapping failure silent
- **Pe2** · Critical node exposure silent
- **Pe3** · Authority-behavior gap silent
- **Pe4** · Champion deficit silent
- **Pe5** · Neutral zone inertia
- **Pe6** · Resistance mismanagement
- **Pe7** · Tracking decay and regression silent

**Subtext:** Five of seven can fail without any obvious warning sign — the highest concentration of silent failure risk across all three layers.

**Notes:** The authority-behavior gap (Pe3) is worth a sentence: a stakeholder who holds a title and does not exercise it is more dangerous than no owner at all, because it creates the appearance of accountability without the substance.

------

### Slide 21 — People: The Cross-Layer Effect

**Title:** People Problems Don't Stay in People

**Content:** Three triggered effects:

- Authority-behavior gap → process decisions go unmade → execution inconsistency goes unchallenged → regression goes unaddressed
- Neutral zone not moving → technically sound system, properly trained staff → adoption fails because the behavioral conditions for adoption were never in place
- Champion burnout → peer reinforcement disappears → post-launch process and technology deterioration follows

**Subtext:** People is not beside Process and Technology as an equal third. It is the behavioral substrate on which the other two operate.

**Notes:** This is the closing argument of the three-layer sequence. The People Layer is the condition that makes the other two possible.

------

## SECTION 6 — SYNTHESIS

*3 slides | ~2.5 minutes*

------

### Slide 22 — Implement: Putting It All Together

**Title:** The Last Step Is Where It Becomes Real

**Content:** Return to the three-step loop, zooming in on Implement:

- Success definitions → embedded as milestone checkpoints, not aspirational targets
- Assumption tracker → live, reviewed continuously, carried past go-live
- Risk register → by layer, by severity, with named owners
- Detection mechanisms → active from day one, transferred to operations at close
- Response protocols → staged before they are needed, reviewed during piloting

**Subtext:** The outputs of the six questions do not belong in a report. They belong in the project plan, running alongside the work.

**Notes:** Echo Slide 8 deliberately — this is the callback. Conference audiences respond to structural repetition that closes a loop.

------

### Slide 23 — The Transfer Test

**Title:** The Project Is Not Done Until Ownership Has Moved

**Content:** Three transfer conditions — all three must be true before the project closes:

- **Documented** — process ownership, detection cadence, and response protocols are formally recorded and handed over
- **Resourced** — the people inheriting ownership have the time, tools, and authority to exercise it
- **Tested** — the transfer has been confirmed, not assumed; the operational team can demonstrate they can run what they are inheriting

**Subtext:** A project that closes without a confirmed transfer has not finished — it has simply stopped being managed.

**Notes:** This is the practical closing test. It is specific enough to be actionable and honest enough to be uncomfortable. That discomfort is useful.

------

### Slide 24 — Closing

**Title:** Failure Is the Default. Success Is What You Build.

**Content:**

> *"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

**Subtext:** The framework does not promise a successful implementation. It gives you the structure to make failure visible early enough to do something about it.

**Notes:** Return to the Core Statement. Close on the premise, not on optimism. Questions welcome.
