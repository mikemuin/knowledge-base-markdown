What's next is exactly what we did with Technology — **define success for the Process layer.** That anchor is what makes the failure control map coherent. Without it, your risks are just a list. With it, every risk is the inversion of something specific.

And given the framing we just did, the Process success definition is going to be more complex than Technology's — because success here isn't a state you reach on go-live day. It's a state you have to engineer through a transition that is inherently unstable.

------

**The trap in defining Process success**

Most programs define Process success as: *the future state workflow is documented, trained, and live.* That is a definition of process *deployment*, not process *success*. A workflow can be documented, trained, and live — and still be failing. Staff execute it inconsistently. The hybrid persists. Informal workarounds re-emerge around the edges. KPIs don't move.

Deployment is an event. Success is a sustained condition.

------

**A better definition**

> *The future state workflow is executing as designed — by actual clinical staff, in real operational conditions — with load-bearing informal processes consciously addressed, the hybrid state compressed to its minimum viable duration, measurable improvement in defined process outcomes achieved, and no regression to the current state.*

Every word is load-bearing. Let's unpack it.

------

**"Executing as designed"**

This means the process on paper matches the process in practice. Not approximately. Not by the champions and super-users. By the full population of staff the process was designed for, across shifts, across sites, under normal and abnormal operating conditions.

This criterion immediately exposes the gap between process *design* and process *reality* — and forces you to close it before you call something successful.

------

**"By actual clinical staff"**

This distinction matters more than it appears. Processes that only work when executed by trained super-users, or by staff in ideal conditions, have not succeeded. They have been demonstrated. Success requires the *median* performer — not the champion — to execute the workflow correctly as a matter of routine.

This is one of the hardest criteria to accept organizationally, because leadership often measures adoption by looking at the best-performing units. The worst-performing unit is where your process failure lives.

------

**"In real operational conditions"**

Not in a training environment. Not in a well-staffed morning shift. In the conditions where clinical care actually happens — short-staffed night shifts, emergency surges, system slowdowns, competing priorities. If the process only works when conditions are favorable, it is fragile by design.

This criterion demands that process design be stress-tested against worst-case operational scenarios, not just optimal ones. In Health IT, this is almost never done.

------

**"Load-bearing informal processes consciously addressed"**

This is the criterion that most programs miss entirely. Recall that every clinical environment runs on a layer of undocumented workarounds and tacit handoffs that have evolved over years. These are not inefficiencies to be eliminated — they are often compensating mechanisms for legitimate gaps in the formal process.

Success requires that you have *found* them, *understood* them, and made a conscious decision about each one: replace it with a formal equivalent, preserve it deliberately, or retire it because the new process genuinely makes it obsolete.

An informal process you didn't find is a ticking clock. It will resurface as a workaround, a data gap, or a care coordination failure — usually within the first ninety days post-go-live.

------

**"Hybrid state compressed to its minimum viable duration"**

You cannot eliminate the hybrid state. The moment you accept that, you stop trying to prevent it and start trying to *manage* it. This criterion acknowledges that the hybrid exists — and demands that it be as short and as controlled as possible.

Compressing the hybrid state requires three things:

- A **cutover strategy** that is definitive, not gradual where gradual is avoidable
- **Clear rules** for what documentation lives where during the transition period
- **Active monitoring** for the signature of hybrid state persistence — split documentation, inconsistent handoffs, parallel paper and digital records

The hybrid state is not a phase. It is a risk condition that must be actively closed.

------

**"Measurable improvement in defined process outcomes"**

This is the anchor to strategic purpose. Process success is not about executing the workflow — it is about what the workflow *produces*. Defined outcomes must be established before the future state is designed, so you can measure improvement against a pre-implementation baseline.

In Health IT, these outcomes might include: referral turnaround time, clinical documentation completeness, medication reconciliation accuracy, care transition quality, or discharge planning efficiency. The specific metrics depend on the process — but they must exist before go-live, not be invented after.

This criterion also catches the most insidious Process failure mode: a workflow that is perfectly executed and produces no improvement. That is a design failure, not an execution failure — and it needs a different response.

------

**"No regression to the current state"**

This is the sustainability criterion. Process change is reversible. Under pressure — new leadership, system downtime, staff turnover, competing priorities — clinical staff revert to familiar behaviors. Success requires that the future state holds over time, not just in the weeks immediately following go-live.

Regression is not dramatic. It is incremental. A form goes back to paper. A handoff reverts to verbal. A documentation step gets skipped. Each small regression is invisible; the cumulative effect is the quiet restoration of the current state inside the shell of the future state.

------

**Putting it together — and connecting to the hybrid state**

Notice what this success definition does structurally. It frames the entire process transition as a compression problem:

- You start with a **misrepresented current state** (more complex, more informal, more variable than documented)
- You design a **future state** that must survive contact with clinical reality
- You manage a **hybrid state** that is your highest-risk operational window
- You reach **sustainable future state execution** when all seven criteria are met

The failure control map will be organized around where that compression can break down.
