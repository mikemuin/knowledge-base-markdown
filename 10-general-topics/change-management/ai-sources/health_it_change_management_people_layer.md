# Health IT Change Management Framework
## People Layer

---

## Overarching Principle

**Change management is the disciplined management of failure within People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — in order to protect the integrity of the transition from current to future state.**

*For the full articulation of the overarching principle, including key clarifications, strategic questions, and the PPT overlay, refer to the Technology Layer document.*

---

## The People Layer

> *People is the Behavior System. What you are managing: stakeholder behavior from start to finish — engagement, ownership, and adoption across the full arc of the project.*

**Character of this layer:** The hardest to manage. The most sensitive. Many hesitate to act.

---

## Why People Is the Hardest Layer

Technology failure is visible — a system goes down, an interface breaks, a report produces wrong numbers. Process failure is observable — a workflow is not being followed, documentation is split, a handoff is missed. People failure is neither. It is behavioral, positional, and political. It is the layer where the most consequential risks are carried by the people least likely to announce them.

People failure does not announce itself. It accumulates.

A stakeholder who was mapped as Passive Support at project initiation and has drifted to Neutral by month four generates no alert. A champion who is burning out withdraws from advocacy before anyone notices the withdrawal. A decision node in Passive Resistance approves everything in steering committee and slow-rolls everything in operation. By the time People failure becomes visible, it has usually been present long enough to have damaged the Process and Technology layers it was supposed to support.

### The Behavioral Coordinate System

People failure management requires a tool that Technology and Process do not need: a way to locate stakeholders in behavioral space and track their movement over time. The stakeholder scale provides this.

### Why People Bleeds Into Everything Else

Every Process domain has a human executing it. Every Technology domain has a human adopting or ignoring it. The ownership annotations throughout the Process map — the observation that having a title is different from exercising authority — are People problems wearing Process labels. The hybrid state persists not because the cutover strategy was poorly designed but because individual stakeholders are making behavioral choices about which version of reality to operate in. Technology workarounds emerge not because the system is unavailable but because the people who should use it have decided not to.

People is not one layer among three equals. It is the layer that determines whether the other two can function.

---

## The Stakeholder Scale

The primary diagnostic and tracking instrument of the People layer. Used as an **ongoing dynamic tool** — not a project initiation snapshot — with a defined assessment cadence throughout the project lifecycle and into post-live sustainment.

| Level | Characteristic | Typical Behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **Active Support** | Champions | Proactively promotes the project; solves problems before they reach the lead |
| **Passive Support** | Happily Allow | Agrees with the goal but will not work for it unless asked |
| **Neutral** | The Middle Ground | Attends meetings without contributing; waiting to see who wins |
| **Passive Resistance** | Delayed Coordination | Slow-rolling tasks, missing deadlines, being too busy to help |
| **Active Resistance** | Loud Anti-Project | Vocal criticism, attempting to divert funding, actively recruiting others to oppose |

### Three Properties of the Scale That Shape the Framework

**The Neutral zone is not safe ground.** Neutrality is a political calculation, not an absence of position. The neutral stakeholder is watching whether resistance or support produces better outcomes. If Active Resistance appears to be winning — creating delays, diverting attention, recruiting others — neutral stakeholders drift downward. If champions are visibly succeeding, neutrals drift upward. Managing Active Resistance is therefore not only about the resistor. It is about the signal resistance sends to everyone watching from the neutral zone.

**Passive Resistance is the highest silent failure risk on the scale.** Active Resistance is visible and triggers a response. Passive Resistance is covert — slow-rolling, missed deadlines, being perpetually too busy — and can be attributed to workload or competing priorities indefinitely. At high organizational authority levels, Passive Resistance is more dangerous than Active Resistance precisely because it is invisible.

**Movement is not guaranteed to be linear or permanent.** A stakeholder who reaches Passive Support can regress to Neutral if they perceive the project losing momentum. A champion who loses organizational protection can disengage. The scale requires regression awareness built in — the same structural discipline applied to regression in the Process layer.

### The Assessment Cadence

The scale must be maintained through three simultaneous rhythms:

- **Time-based:** Regular baseline assessment at defined intervals — monthly minimum during active implementation
- **Event-triggered:** Immediate reassessment following leadership changes, milestone delays, go-live events, public resistance incidents, or any significant organizational change in a participating department
- **Signal-based:** Rapid response when behavioral indicators appear that suggest position movement before it is confirmed — a champion becoming less responsive, a Passive Support stakeholder beginning to vocalize concerns, a neutral zone that was thinning beginning to stabilize

Stakeholder maps built at project initiation and not updated are worse than useless — they create false confidence while the behavioral landscape shifts beneath them.

## The Two-Axis Framework

The stakeholder scale tells you *where* someone is. It does not tell you what they can do from there. Organizational authority determines the consequence of any given scale position. The two axes together produce the operational risk rating.

**Axis 1 — Scale position:** Where the stakeholder sits behaviorally, from Active Resistance to Active Support.

**Axis 2 — Organizational authority:** What the stakeholder controls — decisions, resources, clinical credibility, social influence, network connectivity.

### The Danger Combinations

The most dangerous combination in any Health IT implementation is **high organizational authority + Passive Resistance**. Not Active Resistance — Passive. Active Resistance at high authority is visible and forces a response. Passive Resistance at high authority is invisible: budget lines quietly disappear, staff time that was committed never materializes, approvals are perpetually pending. By the time it is identified, the damage is done.

The second most dangerous combination is **high clinical or social credibility + Active Resistance**. This stakeholder has no formal authority to stop the project — but their public skepticism is being watched by the neutral zone and interpreted as a signal to wait. Their resistance is a broadcast.

---

## Critical Nodes

A critical node is any position in the stakeholder network where the person's scale position disproportionately determines whether the project can move forward. Five types, each with a distinct risk profile on the two axes.

### The Five Node Types

**Decision nodes** — people who control go/no-go decisions at key project milestones: governance approvals, configuration sign-offs, go-live authorization. If they sit below Passive Support, decisions get delayed, qualified, or quietly reversed. Organizational authority is the primary risk axis.

**Resource nodes** — people who control budget, staff time, infrastructure access, or system permissions. Passive Resistance here does not look like opposition — it looks like *"we don't have the bandwidth right now"* — indefinitely. This is one of the purest expressions of slow-rolling in the entire scale. Organizational authority is the primary risk axis.

**Influence nodes** — people whose opinion disproportionately shapes where others sit on the scale. They may not have formal authority, but they carry social authority — and in clinical environments, social authority routinely outweighs organizational authority. The senior consultant who has practiced at the hospital for twenty years and is skeptical of the new system is an influence node. Their position on the scale is being watched by the neutral zone. Scale position is the primary risk axis.

**Workflow nodes** — people who sit at critical handoffs in the clinical or operational process. Their adoption or resistance directly determines whether the Process layer succeeds at that handoff. A ward clerk at a critical documentation junction in Passive Resistance can quietly undermine a workflow that everyone else is executing correctly. Both axes matter equally.

**Network nodes** — connectors. People who communicate across silos, departments, or professional boundaries. Often not the most senior person in the room — but the most connected. A champion at a network node has disproportionate upward influence on the neutral zone because they reach people that formal communication channels never touch. This is the highest-leverage champion placement available.

### Node Risk Rating

| Node Type | Primary Risk Axis | Danger Condition |
|---|---|---|
| Decision | Organizational authority | High authority + below Passive Support |
| Resource | Organizational authority | Controls budget or time + Passive Resistance |
| Influence | Scale position | Active Resistance + high clinical or social credibility |
| Workflow | Both equally | Any resistance at a mandatory process handoff |
| Network | Scale position | Active Resistance + high connectivity |

## Champion Management

Champions are the primary mechanism for moving the neutral zone. Not communication plans. Not training programs. Not executive mandates. The person in the Neutral zone who is waiting to see who wins is most reliably moved by a peer they respect who has already decided.

This makes champion management a strategic function — not a passive benefit.

### Champion Cultivation Is an Active Intervention

Champions are almost always treated as a discovery — you find willing advocates and are grateful for them. In a well-designed People framework, champion cultivation is deliberate:

- Identify *potential* champions early — people with credibility, reach, and upward mobility on the scale
- Create the conditions that move them to Active Support
- Deploy them strategically against a coverage map — not randomly, not wherever they are most willing
- Protect them from organizational pressure that pushes them back
- Manage their load, because champions burn out when over-relied upon and under-supported

### The Champion Profile

Willingness alone is not a sufficient selection criterion. A champion should bring:

- **Credibility** with the specific peer group they will influence
- **Connectivity** — access to people and networks the formal project structure cannot reach
- **Upward mobility** on the scale — they are genuinely moving toward and sustaining Active Support, not performing it
- **Availability** — sustained, not episodic

### Champion Distribution Is a Strategic Placement Problem

Champions are not interchangeable. A champion in the informatics team does not cover the clinical operations gap. The question is not how many champions you have — it is whether every critical node and every significant concentration of neutral stakeholders has a credible, connected peer signal reaching them.

The champion network should be resilient to the loss of any single member. If any one champion's departure would create a critical coverage gap, the network is under-resourced.

---

## People Success Definition

> *The People layer succeeds when enough stakeholders of sufficient authority are positioned at Passive Support or above — with active champions distributed at critical nodes across the organizational network — to sustain forward project momentum through resistance, absorb setbacks without regression, and progressively move the neutral zone upward as the project matures.*

Each element is load-bearing.

---

**"Enough stakeholders of sufficient authority"**

Not all stakeholders. Not universal buy-in. That is an unachievable standard and a distraction. The question is whether you have critical mass at the right positions in the organizational hierarchy. This is where the two-axis framework becomes the operational definition of success — the right scale positions held by people with the right authority to exercise them.

---

**"Distributed at critical nodes"**

Champion coverage is a strategic placement problem, not a headcount problem. Success requires that every critical node type — decision, resource, influence, workflow, network — is occupied by someone at or moving toward Active Support, and that the neutral zone has credible peer signals reaching it from within.

---

**"Sustain forward momentum through resistance"**

Resistance is not a failure condition — it is a constant. The People layer has succeeded not when resistance is eliminated but when the project can absorb it without losing momentum. This requires a resistance management capability, not just a resistance management plan.

---

**"Absorb setbacks without regression"**

Setbacks — a delayed go-live, a failed pilot, a public criticism — test the resilience of the behavioral landscape. A People layer that has been well-managed holds through setbacks because the stakeholder positions are genuine, not performative. A People layer built on surface compliance collapses under the first significant pressure.

---

**"Progressively move the neutral zone upward"**

The neutral zone is the largest addressable population and the most important leverage point in the People layer. Success is measured by how much of the neutral zone has moved to Passive Support by go-live, and how much Passive Support has moved to Active Support by ninety days post-live. The direction and rate of movement in the neutral zone is the leading indicator of People layer health.

## People Failure Control Map

Seven failure domains organized around two structural failure types: **positioning failures** — stakeholders stuck at the wrong scale position for the role they need to play — and **movement failures** — stakeholders who were moving upward and stopped, regressed, or were never tracked.

> ⚠ indicates **high silent failure risk** — failure most likely to be invisible before it becomes irreversible or normalized.

---

### Domain 1 · Stakeholder Mapping Failure
**Failure mode:** You do not know who is where, why they are there, or what authority they hold. You are managing a project without a map.

This is the People equivalent of current state misrepresentation in the Process layer. Everything built on top of it inherits the error.

#### Top Risks
- Incomplete stakeholder identification — the formal register captures who attends steering committees; it misses the influence node three levels down whose opinion moves the neutral zone
- Position assessed by assumption, not evidence — stakeholders assigned a scale position based on role, seniority, or prior relationship rather than observed behavior; silence treated as Passive Support when it is frequently Neutral or early Passive Resistance
- The why never diagnosed — position mapped without understanding why the person sits there; without the why, no intervention can be correctly designed
- Static mapping — the stakeholder map built at project initiation treated as current for the duration of the project; a living behavioral landscape managed as a historical document

#### Prevent
- Structured stakeholder discovery that goes beyond the org chart — explicitly ask: *"Who has blocked projects like this before? Who do people listen to when they are uncertain? Who controls the resources this project depends on?"*
- Behavioral evidence as the basis for scale positioning — what has this person said, done, or not done that tells you where they sit? Position justified by observation, not inferred from title
- Two-axis mapping from the beginning — every stakeholder placed on both dimensions: scale position and organizational authority; the combination produces the risk rating
- The why documented for every stakeholder below Passive Support — as a hypothesis to be tested and refined, not as a final judgment

#### Detect
- Surprises at key decision points — a stakeholder mapped as Passive Support who blocks a milestone indicates the position was assessed incorrectly
- Resistance appearing from unmapped stakeholders — the clearest signal that identification was incomplete

#### Respond
- Immediate map update when a mapping failure is detected — root cause: was the position wrong, or did it change?
- Rapid stakeholder engagement to diagnose the why
- Re-segmentation of the full stakeholder landscape if the gap is significant

**Owner:** Change Manager / Project Sponsor
*Annotation: The Change Manager owns the methodology. The Project Sponsor must validate the map at the authority level — because some stakeholder positions are only visible to someone operating at the same organizational level.*

---

### Domain 2 · Critical Node Exposure
**Failure mode:** A critical node is held by someone whose scale position or authority level makes them a structural threat to project momentum

This is the highest-consequence positioning failure. It is not about managing a difficult stakeholder. It is about a fundamental mismatch between organizational position and behavioral commitment.

#### Top Risks
- Decision node below Passive Support — a governance member or clinical lead who controls milestone decisions and sits in Neutral or below; decisions made from this position are deferred decisions, qualified approvals, or formalized delay mechanisms
- Resource node in Passive Resistance — the slow-roll; budget requests taking longer than they should, committed staff time that never materializes, infrastructure access perpetually pending
- Influence node in Active Resistance — a high-credibility clinician or operational leader whose public skepticism is being watched by the neutral zone and interpreted as a signal to wait; their resistance is a broadcast
- Unoccupied critical nodes — not a resistance problem but an absence problem; no one of credibility and connectivity is positioned to influence the stakeholders around a critical node

#### Prevent
- Critical node identification as a formal early project workstream — name each node, identify who occupies it, assess their two-axis position, and make a conscious management decision about each before the project reaches its first key milestone
- Targeted champion placement at unoccupied or under-championed critical nodes — identify the gap, cultivate the champion, deploy deliberately
- Early engagement with high-authority stakeholders below Passive Support — before key milestones, not at them; the decision node is most moveable before a decision is required of them

#### Detect
- Milestone delays concentrated at specific decision or resource nodes — the pattern, not the individual event, is the signal; one delayed approval is noise, three from the same node is a positioning problem
- The neutral zone not moving upward in areas covered by an influence node in Active Resistance — their broadcast is working

#### Respond
- Direct executive engagement for high-authority nodes below Passive Support — the Project Sponsor's intervention, not the Change Manager's; peer-to-peer at the same organizational level
- Champion insertion at unoccupied nodes — identify, cultivate, place; if the node is on the critical path, this is measured in days, not weeks
- Resistance reframing for influence nodes: engage publicly in the forum where their resistance is being broadcast — not to defeat them but to demonstrate that the project can absorb challenge and respond with substance; this is what the neutral zone is watching for

**Owner:** Project Sponsor / Executive Leadership
*Annotation: Critical node exposure at the authority level cannot be managed by the Change Manager or project team. It requires someone who operates at the same organizational level as the exposed node.*

---

### Domain 3 · Authority-Behavior Gap
**Failure mode:** The person with the title does not exercise the authority; they are formally accountable and behaviorally absent or resistant

This is the People layer's full explanation of the ownership annotation that runs through the Process map. Having a title is different from exercising authority. The authority-behavior gap is where that distinction is managed — or not.

#### Top Risks
- Nominal ownership — a clinical lead assigned as process owner or governance member who is in the Neutral zone; they attend, they do not contribute, they do not decide; they occupy the seat without exercising its authority, which means no one else can occupy it either
- Delegated resistance — a senior leader personally in Passive or Active Resistance who delegates project involvement to a supportive subordinate; the subordinate has no authority to move anything; the resistance is shielded from view by the delegation structure
- Conditional support — a stakeholder who expresses support in steering committee and exercises resistance through operational decisions; *"yes in the room, no in the field"*; creates contradictory signals that the neutral zone interprets as project weakness

#### Prevent
- Behavioral expectations made explicit at project initiation for every stakeholder with formal accountability — specific, not general; *"Your role requires you to: sign off on configuration decisions by these dates, communicate project direction to your team by these milestones, resolve workflow disputes that escalate to your level within these timeframes"*
- Authority exercise mapped alongside scale position in the two-axis tracking tool — not just where someone is on the scale, but whether they are exercising the authority their position requires

#### Detect
- A decision formally assigned to a stakeholder not made by the required date without a legitimate reason — pattern-match across multiple instances
- Escalations arriving at the project level that should have been resolved at a specific authority level — the project team absorbing decisions that belong to clinical or operational ownership
- The yes-in-the-room, no-in-the-field pattern — tracked by comparing steering committee posture to operational behavior during the same period

#### Respond
- Structured conversation between the Project Sponsor and the stakeholder — naming the gap explicitly in terms of project risk, not personal criticism: *"The project needs X from your role. We are not getting X. What is preventing it?"*
- Role restructure if the gap is structural — reassign formal accountability to someone who will exercise it; give the original stakeholder a role that matches their actual commitment level

**Owner:** Project Sponsor
*Annotation: Addressing an authority-behavior gap requires someone with higher organizational standing than the stakeholder in question. This is unambiguously the Project Sponsor's domain and cannot be delegated to the project team.*

---

### Domain 4 · Champion Deficit
**Failure mode:** Insufficient champions, wrong distribution, over-reliance on too few, or champion burnout before the project reaches sustainability

#### Top Risks
- Headcount substituted for distribution — enough champions in aggregate but concentrated in the same department, shift, or professional group; the neutral zone in uncovered areas receives no peer signal
- Champions discovered, not cultivated — the project relies on finding willing volunteers; the result is a network that reflects who was already enthusiastic, not who occupies the most strategically important nodes
- Champion over-reliance — two or three champions doing the work of ten; visible, committed, credible, and burning out; champion fatigue manifests as withdrawal from advocacy before it manifests as anything visible
- Champions without organizational protection — a champion who faces peer pressure, managerial skepticism, or political consequence for their advocacy will disengage; not because they stopped believing in the project, but because the personal cost became unsustainable

#### Prevent
- Champion cultivation as a formal project workstream with a strategic placement map — identify coverage gaps against the critical node map and the neutral zone geography; cultivate champions to fill specific gaps, not recruit generically
- Selection criteria beyond willingness: credibility with the specific peer group to be influenced, connectivity across the relevant network, upward mobility on the scale, sustained availability
- Distributed load: enough champions that no individual is indispensable; the network must be resilient to the loss of any single member
- Organizational protection for champions: visible executive acknowledgment of their role, time allocation built into their workplan, a clear escalation path when they face pushback; champions who know their advocacy is backed sustain their commitment
- Champion-specific engagement: regular check-ins, feedback loops from champion experience back into project decisions, explicit recognition; champions who feel heard stay engaged; champions who feel used disengage

#### Detect
- Champion network coverage map reviewed at every tracking cadence — which critical nodes are covered, which are thinning, which are unoccupied
- Champion engagement declining — less frequent communication, shorter responses, withdrawal from voluntary activities; the earliest behavioral signal of fatigue
- Neutral zone not moving in specific areas of the organization — often indicates champion absence or disengagement in that area, not resistance

#### Respond
- Rapid recruitment and cultivation when coverage gaps are identified — targeted to the specific gap, not broadcast; the right person for the specific node
- Load redistribution when fatigue signals appear — reduce the ask on burning champions before they withdraw, not after
- Executive re-engagement with the champion network when organizational pressure is driving disengagement; public, visible backing from leadership is the most effective protection a champion can receive

**Owner:** Change Manager with executive backing
*Annotation: The Change Manager designs and manages the champion network. Champion protection requires executive visibility. Without the second, the first is insufficient.*

---

### Domain 5 · Neutral Zone Inertia
**Failure mode:** The largest addressable population is not moving; the "waiting to see who wins" calculation is not resolving in the project's favor

This is the highest-leverage domain in the entire People map. The neutral zone is the largest population. Moving it upward even partially has more impact on project momentum than converting a single Active Resistor. The primary mechanism for movement is peer influence — not communication plans, not training programs, not executive mandates.

#### Top Risks
- Resistance appearing to win — if Active Resistance is visible, vocal, and appears to be producing results, the neutral calculation tips toward waiting longer or toward quiet alignment with resistance; managing Active Resistance is not only about the resistor, it is about the signal resistance sends to the neutral zone
- No compelling personal reason to move — the neutral stakeholder sees no specific benefit to moving upward and no cost to staying neutral; generic communication about what the project does has not told them what it means for them specifically
- Absence of credible peer signals — the neutral zone responds most strongly to people like them — same role, same professional identity, same organizational level — who have already moved upward; without peer signals, the only available information comes from the project team, which is discounted
- Silence misread as acceptance — the neutral zone is quiet; the project team interprets this as non-resistance; it is a population waiting for more information before committing; treating it as passive acceptance means no intervention and no movement

#### Prevent
- Champion placement in and around the neutral zone — specifically at network nodes and influence nodes that neutral stakeholders respect and watch; peer signal is the primary mechanism of upward movement
- Targeted, role-specific communication: what does this change mean for a ward nurse specifically, for a charge physician specifically, for an administrative coordinator specifically; generic benefits statements do not move neutral stakeholders; specific, credible, personally relevant ones do
- Active resistance managed visibly and quickly — not to punish, but to signal to the neutral zone that resistance is not the winning calculation; when a project responds to vocal resistance with substance and momentum, the neutral zone receives a different signal

#### Detect
- Neutral zone size not decreasing as the project matures — by specific milestones, a defined proportion of neutral stakeholders should have moved to Passive Support; tracking this proportion is a direct measure of neutral zone health
- Neutral stakeholders beginning to vocalize skepticism — the pre-signal of downward movement toward Passive Resistance; the neutral calculation is tipping
- Uptake rates on optional project activities — training sign-ups, feedback surveys, pilot participation; low uptake from the neutral zone is inertia made measurable

#### Respond
- Peer engagement campaign activated when the neutral zone is not moving — structured, not broadcast; identify which champions have the strongest relationships in the unmoved neutral population and deploy them specifically
- Direct engagement with neutral stakeholders to diagnose their calculation — what would it take to move them? what concern has not been addressed? this is an information-gathering exercise, not a persuasion exercise; the answer shapes the intervention
- Visible project wins communicated in terms the neutral zone cares about — momentum is persuasive; evidence that the project is succeeding gives the neutral calculation a new data point

**Owner:** Change Manager / Champion Network
*Annotation: The neutral zone moves most reliably through peer influence, not formal project communication. The Change Manager designs the strategy. The champion network executes it. Neither works without the other.*

### Domain 6 · Resistance Mismanagement
**Failure mode:** Resistance identified but wrong intervention applied because the why was never diagnosed; the response pushes the stakeholder further down the scale

This is the most avoidable failure in the People map — and among the most common.

#### Top Risks
- Communication applied to a concern that requires engagement — a stakeholder in Passive Resistance because they feel excluded from decisions receives more project newsletters; their concern is about voice and participation; more information about a project they have no input into reinforces the problem
- Escalation applied to a stakeholder who needs listening — a clinician in Active Resistance because of a legitimate unaddressed workflow concern is reported to executive leadership as an obstacle; the escalation confirms their belief that the project team is not interested in their input; they recruit three others
- Reassurance applied to a credibility concern — a stakeholder who does not believe the project will succeed receives repeated assurances that it will; assurances without evidence deepen skepticism; evidence addresses credibility concerns, words do not
- Legitimate resistance ignored — sometimes Active Resistance is correct; the stakeholder has identified a genuine problem — a workflow that does not work, a data quality issue, a resource gap — and is expressing it badly; the intervention is not to manage the resistance but to address the underlying concern and convert the resistor into an informed contributor

#### The Why Diagnosis — Required Before Any Intervention

Five consistent why categories in Health IT, each requiring a different response:

| Why | Diagnosis Signal | Intervention |
|---|---|---|
| Unaddressed legitimate concern | Can articulate a specific technical or workflow objection | Address the concern, not the resistance |
| Exclusion from decisions | References lack of consultation, feels bypassed | Structured involvement in relevant decisions |
| Political alignment with a senior resistor | Resistance tracks closely with a more senior resistor's position | Address the senior node; the aligned resistance may resolve |
| Credibility doubt — project will not succeed | References past failed implementations | Evidence-based engagement: pilots, wins, transparent progress reporting |
| Protecting a resource or workflow dependency | Resistance concentrated around specific scope areas | Negotiate; acknowledge the dependency; find a path that does not require sacrifice |

#### Prevent
- The why diagnosis as a required step before any resistance intervention is designed — not a courtesy, not a formality; the single most important determinant of whether the intervention will work
- Interventions designed against the diagnosed why, not against the surface behavior

#### Detect
- Resistance intensifying after an intervention — the clearest signal that the wrong intervention was applied; track scale position before and after each engagement action
- Resistance spreading to previously neutral stakeholders — the intervention became visible and signaled that resistance produces engagement, making it an attractive strategy for others

#### Respond
- Re-diagnose before re-intervening — what did the failed intervention reveal about the actual why?
- Apply the corrected intervention and allow adequate time; behavioral movement is not instantaneous
- Build in the expectation that some stakeholders will not move, and design the project's risk tolerance around that reality; the goal is not conversion of every resistor — it is sufficient critical mass above Passive Support to sustain momentum

**Owner:** Change Manager
*Annotation: The why diagnosis requires skill, not just authority. This domain is most dependent on change management competence — a politically sophisticated Change Manager who can diagnose behavioral dynamics is not interchangeable with a project coordinator who can run a communication plan.*

### Domain 7 · Tracking Decay and Regression
**Failure mode:** The stakeholder map was accurate at project initiation and never updated; the behavioral landscape shifted; the project team did not see it; the map says the project is safe and it is not

#### Top Risks
- Assessment cadence abandoned after initiation — the kickoff map was thorough and useful; four months later it has not been updated; leadership changes, political shifts, go-live stress, and unaddressed concerns have moved multiple stakeholders invisibly
- Event triggers not defined — the team reassesses on a schedule but does not know to reassess when a senior leadership change occurs, when a go-live is delayed, when a public resistance event happens, or when a champion disengages
- Champion regression undetected — a champion who was at Active Support at month two is at Passive Support at month five; their advocacy has become less frequent, less energetic, less visible; the project team still counts them as Active Support; the coverage map is wrong
- Post-go-live tracking abandoned — stakeholder management treated as a pre-go-live activity; after go-live the tracking stops; regression in the People layer is incremental and invisible without a monitoring structure

#### Prevent
- A defined tracking cadence operating simultaneously across three rhythms: time-based (regular baseline assessment), event-triggered (immediate reassessment at defined trigger events), and signal-based (rapid response when behavioral pre-signals appear)
- Event triggers defined at project initiation: leadership changes, milestone delays, go-live events, public resistance incidents, champion disengagement signals, significant organizational changes in participating departments
- Champion-specific tracking maintained separately from general stakeholder tracking — champion health (engagement level, advocacy frequency, organizational protection) monitored at every cadence
- Post-go-live stakeholder tracking built into the sustainment plan — ninety-day and six-month reassessments as a minimum; not dropped at project closure

#### Detect
- The delta between current map and previous assessment at every cadence — not where people are, but where they moved to and in which direction; the delta is the signal
- Behavioral leading indicators that predict position change before the position changes: a champion becoming less responsive, a Passive Support stakeholder beginning to vocalize concerns, a neutral zone that was thinning beginning to stabilize or grow; these are the signals that matter most

#### Respond
- Rapid re-engagement when downward movement is detected early — the cost of intervention rises steeply as regression deepens; a stakeholder who has moved from Passive Support to Neutral can often be re-engaged with a single meaningful conversation; a stakeholder who has moved from Neutral to Active Resistance requires a sustained campaign
- Map correction as an immediate output of any response action — the map must reflect current reality at all times, including the uncertainty about positions that have not been recently assessed
- If widespread regression is detected post-go-live, a formal re-engagement plan structured as a project — with defined milestones, accountability, and executive sponsorship — not treated as a routine operational correction

**Owner:** Change Manager — sustained through project closure and into operational sustainment
*Annotation: This is the only People domain where ownership is unambiguous and singular. The Change Manager owns the tracking instrument, the cadence, and the response logic. If the Change Manager role ends at go-live, this domain fails by design.*

## Key Cross-Cutting Observations

### The Ownership Pattern

The consistent pattern across all seven People domains: the Change Manager owns the methodology; the Project Sponsor owns the authority interventions. When one operates without the other, the People layer has structural gaps that no amount of tactical skill can close.

| Domain | What Is Required | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Stakeholder mapping | Diagnostic skill + executive access | Change Manager + Project Sponsor |
| Critical node exposure | Peer-level authority | Executive Leadership / Project Sponsor |
| Authority-behavior gap | Hierarchical authority | Project Sponsor |
| Champion deficit | Network design + executive backing | Change Manager + Executive |
| Neutral zone inertia | Peer influence | Champion Network |
| Resistance mismanagement | Behavioral diagnostic skill | Change Manager |
| Tracking decay | Sustained discipline + mandate | Change Manager |

### The Silent Failure Concentration

Five of seven People domains carry silent failure risk — the highest concentration across all three layers. People failure is structurally the most silent because human behavior is the least instrumented dimension of any project. A dropped interface message can be detected by a monitoring alert. A stakeholder drifting from Passive Support to Neutral generates no alert. Detection in the People layer requires deliberate human observation and a maintained tracking discipline, not system instrumentation.

### People Does Not End at Go-Live

The Technology layer has a go-live date. The Process layer has a future state target. The People layer has neither — because behavioral commitment, champion engagement, and neutral zone movement do not resolve on a project timeline. The stakeholder landscape continues to shift after go-live. Champions burn out. New leadership changes the political environment. Staff turnover replaces trained advocates with untrained newcomers who inherit the informal behavioral norms of whoever trained them.

The People failure control map must be read as a sustained management discipline, not a project-phase deliverable. The tracking cadence, the champion network, and the resistance management capability do not end at go-live. They transition — from the project team to the operational management structure — in exactly the same way that process ownership must be transferred before the project closes.

### The Relationship Between People and the Other Layers

People does not sit beside Process and Technology as an equal third. It is the behavioral substrate on which the other two operate. A technically sound system deployed into a field of Passive Resistance will fail. A well-designed process owned by nominal champions will regress.
