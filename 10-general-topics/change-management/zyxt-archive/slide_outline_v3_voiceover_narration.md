# Voice-Over Narration Script

## Failure-Aware Change Management Framework

**Format:** Video lecture voice-over **Slides:** 24 **Estimated runtime:** 19-22 minutes

------

## SECTION 1 — INTRODUCTION

------

### Slide 1 — Title

*Why Health IT Projects Fail — And What To Do About It*

This framework comes from field experience: Health IT projects that succeeded, projects that failed, and the more dangerous category — projects that failed quietly enough that no one could agree they had failed at all.

This is not a theory of change management from a conference room. It is about what happens inside hospitals when systems, workflows, and clinical behavior all have to change at the same time.

------

### Slide 2 — The Problem

*The Pattern That Keeps Repeating*

Three statements appear again and again after Health IT projects disappoint.

"The system went live on time, but staff never really adopted it."

"The process looked right on paper, but that is not how anyone actually worked."

"Leadership was supportive, until they weren't."

These sound like three different failures. They are not. They are the same failure wearing different clothes.

The pattern repeats because most change management approaches are built to manage success. They are less good at managing the failure that is already forming under the surface.

------

### Slide 3 — The Central Premise

*This Framework Starts From the Opposite Assumption*

Most change frameworks are optimistic by design. They assume that if the right steps are followed, adoption will happen. Resistance is treated as the exception.

This framework starts somewhere less comfortable.

Failure is the default.

Not because the people are incompetent. Not because the technology is bad. Not because the organization does not care. Failure is the default because clinical environments are complex, busy, political, and unforgiving.

Success is not what happens when nothing goes wrong. Success is what gets deliberately built against the forces that are always working to undo it.

------

### Slide 4 — The Silent Failure Problem

*14 of 20 Ways This Can Fail Generate No Alert*

In most projects, failure announces itself. A missed deadline shows up on the plan. A budget overrun appears in the report. A system outage fires an alert.

In Health IT change management, the most consequential failures often make no noise.

A stakeholder who was supportive in month one drifts into disengagement by month four. No alert fires. A clinical champion starts to withdraw. No report captures it. Data begins flowing incorrectly between two systems that both appear to be working. No dashboard turns red.

This framework identifies twenty ways a Health IT implementation can fail. Fourteen carry a high risk of silent failure.

So the important question is not only, "Where could we fail?" The more neglected question is, "How will we know something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?"

That question is uncomfortable. It requires looking for evidence that the project is not working, at the exact moment when the organization wants proof that it is.

------

### Slide 5 — The Framework at a Glance

*Core Statement. Governing Principles. Three Layers.*

The framework starts with one sentence: the Core Statement.

*"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

There are two phrases to hold onto.

"Concurrent with project execution" means change management starts on day one, not after go-live.

"Integrity of the transition" means the real goal is not launch. The real goal is a stable operating state after the launch.

From that Core Statement come five Governing Principles.

First: **Start on day one, not day done.** If change management starts after configuration, the most important decisions have already been made.

Second: **Engineer for failure, not for optimism.** Assume the plan will break somewhere, then design the controls before it does.

Third: **What you can't see will hurt you most.** Silent failure needs active detection, not passive reporting.

Fourth: **If everyone owns it, no one owns it.** Accountability for the transition has to be assigned. It will not appear by goodwill.

Fifth: **Not all failure costs the same.** A training gap is recoverable. A patient safety event is not. The response has to match the consequence.

The framework then works across three layers: People, Process, and Technology. These are not silos. They are three surfaces of the same transition.

------

## SECTION 2 — HOW TO USE THE FRAMEWORK

------

### Slide 6 — The Three-Step Loop

*Identify. Question. Implement.*

The framework is used in a simple loop: identify, question, implement.

**Identify** means taking inventory. Who is involved? What workflows are changing? Which systems, data flows, and infrastructure are in scope?

**Question** means running the same six questions against that inventory.

**Implement** means turning the answers into live project controls: checkpoints, trackers, detection schedules, prevention tasks, and response protocols.

The important point is that this is not a workshop exercise. It repeats at every major milestone, through go-live, and into sustainment.

------

### Slide 7 — The Six Questions

*The Same Six Questions. Applied to Everything.*

You do not need to memorize the six questions. Listen for the pattern: define success, expose assumptions, identify failure, detect it early, prevent what you can, and prepare for what still breaks.

First: **What does success look like?** It has to be specific enough that two people can agree whether it happened.

Second: **What are we taking for granted?** Every unconfirmed assumption is a risk wearing a nicer name.

Third: **Where could we fail?** Sort the answers by consequence: low, high, and irreversible.

Fourth: **How will we know if something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?** This is the question most teams skip. It requires data, observation, conversations, behavioral signals, a cadence, and a named owner.

Fifth: **What do we do now to reduce the chance of failure?** Prevention without an owner and deadline is only intention.

Sixth: **If something fails, what do we do?** The response needs a trigger, steps, an owner, and a recovery target. For patient safety risks, the first step is containment. Stop the harm first. Understand it second.

------

### Slide 8 — Who Does This Work

*Two Types of Ownership. Both Are Required.*

The framework depends on two kinds of ownership.

The Change Manager owns the methodology: the instruments, the detection cadence, the analysis, and the escalation.

But the Change Manager cannot close problems that require organizational authority. They cannot physically close a transition period, remove a nominal owner, or mandate a clinical practice standard across a department.

That authority sits with the Project Sponsor and executive leadership. Their job is not to run the methodology. Their job is to act on what the methodology surfaces.

A Change Manager without executive backing can see the permanent transition period and have no power to close it. A Project Sponsor without a functioning methodology has no reliable signal that the transition has become permanent.

Both are required. Neither substitutes for the other.

------

### Slide 9 — The Framework Does Not End at Go-Live

*The Project Ends. The Framework Doesn't.*

Go-live ends the launch phase. It does not end the work.

The implementation team may leave. The milestone plan may close. The intense pre-launch activity may stop.

But the detection cadence, assumption tracking, stakeholder monitoring, process compliance checks, and system health reviews must continue for at least twelve months.

The framework ends only when ownership has been formally transferred to operations, and when the receiving teams have the tools, knowledge, and authority to keep running what they have inherited.

Not when the transfer is announced. When it is verified.

------

## SECTION 3 — TECHNOLOGY LAYER

------

### Slide 10 — Technology: What We're Managing

*Technology — The Enablement System*

The Technology Layer is the most visible layer. It has a vendor, a name, a launch date, and a price tag.

That visibility creates overconfidence.

A system that exists is not the same as a system that works. A system that is running is not the same as a system that is being used correctly, consistently, and safely.

Technology success means the right system is in place, reliably accessible, easy enough that staff choose to use it, and measurably improving the work it was meant to support.

And that has to remain true after launch day.

------

### Slide 11 — The Go-Live Trap

*Organisations Declare Victory at Precisely the Wrong Moment*

The most dangerous assumption in Health IT is that going live means the technology has succeeded.

After launch, support begins to withdraw. Small issues accumulate. Staff find workarounds for friction points. Those workarounds become habits.

Months later, what looks like a technology failure has often been building since the week after launch.

The real measure is adoption. Not whether the system can be used, but whether it is being used as intended, by the right staff, across real conditions.

A system that staff work around has failed, no matter how smoothly it launched.

------

### Slide 12 — Six Technology Failure Areas

*Where Technology Actually Fails*

There are six technology failure areas. Again, do not try to memorize the list. Listen for the pattern: the system can be present, available, and still clinically unreliable.

First: **wrong system selection.** The system does not fit how staff actually work.

Second: **access and infrastructure.** Staff cannot reliably reach the system where the work happens.

Third: **data accuracy and system connections.** This is the highest-risk area. Incorrect data can directly affect patient safety, and the failure may be invisible until someone acts on the wrong information.

Fourth: **staff not using the system.** The system is available, but too slow, too cumbersome, or too disconnected from real workflows.

Fifth: **the system does not improve the work.** It runs, but the promised improvement never appears.

Sixth: **decline after launch.** The system works at launch, then deteriorates as support withdraws and small problems compound.

Four of these six can fail silently. That is why technology monitoring cannot stop at uptime.

------

### Slide 13 — Technology: The Cross-Layer Effect

*Technology Problems Don't Stay in Technology*

Technology problems do not stay technical for long.

When data accuracy fails, staff stop trusting the system. They create paper logs, manual checks, and informal safeguards. The process becomes inconsistent across the organization.

When staff stop using the system, the work splits. Some of it is in the system. Some of it is outside. No one has a complete picture of any given patient.

When a system is selected without genuine staff input, trust is damaged early. People who might have supported the project become resistant before the project has even reached its hardest phase.

So if a technology problem keeps returning, do not assume the cause is technical. It may be sitting in Process or People.

------

## SECTION 4 — PROCESS LAYER

------

### Slide 14 — Process: What We're Managing

*Process — The Execution System*

The Process Layer manages the transition from how work is done today to how it needs to be done after the change.

Every clinical environment has two versions of every process.

There is the official version: the policy, the flowchart, the version leadership believes is happening.

Then there is the real version: what staff actually do under time pressure, during short-staffed shifts, and in the gaps the formal process never solved.

The gap between those two versions is where implementations fall apart.

Process success is not a signed-off workflow. It is the future-state workflow being executed correctly by actual clinical staff, in real conditions, with workarounds addressed, improvement measured, and no quiet regression.

------

### Slide 15 — The Transition Period

*The Most Dangerous Operational State in Any Implementation*

Hold onto this phrase: transition period.

This is the interval where old and new processes run at the same time.

Some records are in the new system. Some are on paper. Some staff follow the new process. Some follow the old one. No one has a complete picture of any given patient.

This is where information gets lost, handoffs fail, and errors occur. Not because the system crashed, but because two versions of reality are running at once.

Within about thirty days, if both processes are still running side by side, staff stop treating the situation as temporary. It becomes normal.

A controlled step backward is recoverable. A normalized transition period is not. It is a failed implementation dressed as a work in progress.

------

### Slide 16 — Seven Process Failure Areas

*Where Process Actually Fails*

There are seven process failure areas. The pattern is simple: the process can look clean in governance and still fail on the floor.

First: **getting the current picture wrong.** The new process is designed against the official story, not the real work.

Second: **designing a process that cannot survive reality.** It works in planning and fails under short staffing, high demand, or system slowdown.

Third: **the transition period becomes permanent.** This is the organizing risk of the Process Layer.

Fourth: **missing unofficial workarounds.** These are not just inefficiencies. Often, they are compensating mechanisms for gaps in the formal process.

Fifth: **the process works for champions, but not for everyone.** Aggregate compliance hides the weak unit, the night shift, or the reluctant department.

Sixth: **the process works but produces no improvement.** Compliance is achieved, but outcomes stay flat.

Seventh: **sliding back to the old way of working.** The future state erodes after the project team leaves.

Five of these seven can fail silently. Floor reality has to be checked directly. The spreadsheet will not tell the whole truth.

------

### Slide 17 — Process: The Cross-Layer Effect

*Process Problems Don't Stay in Process*

Process problems spread quickly.

If the current state picture is wrong, the system gets configured against a false foundation. At deployment, the mismatch looks like a technology problem. It began as a process failure.

If the transition period normalizes, data splits between old and new ways of working. The system may be technically operational, but clinically untrustworthy.

If the process regresses, the people who championed it lose credibility. Their advocacy weakens. The People Layer begins to erode.

This is why governance matters. Getting the current picture right requires front-line access. Designing a survivable workflow requires clinical authority. Closing the transition requires operational authority.

If governance cannot activate the right authority at the right moment, the failure control map is not a management tool. It is paperwork.

------

## SECTION 5 — PEOPLE LAYER

------

### Slide 18 — People: What We're Managing

*People — The Behavior System*

Technology and process can look stable on a dashboard. People cannot.

The People Layer manages where each person stands in relation to the change, and whether they are moving in the direction the project needs.

Technology failure is visible. A system goes down. Someone notices.

Process failure is observable. A handoff is missed. Documentation is split. Evidence appears.

People failure is neither. A stakeholder drifts from support to disengagement. A champion burns out. A senior leader approves in governance and slow-rolls in operations.

By formal measures, that leader may still look supportive. Operationally, they are an obstacle.

And unlike the other layers, People has no finish line. Behavior does not resolve on a project timeline.

------

### Slide 19 — The Neutral Zone

*The Largest Addressable Population Is Waiting*

In most Health IT implementations, the largest group is neither actively resisting nor actively supporting.

They are watching.

They watch project momentum. They watch the behavior of people they respect. They watch whether leadership is serious after the first pressure point.

Moving even part of this neutral zone toward passive support matters more than converting one active resistor.

What moves them is usually not another email, another training module, or another executive message.

They are asking a social question: who has already decided, and do I trust that person's judgment?

That is why champion placement matters more than champion headcount. Ten enthusiastic advocates in informatics do not cover a gap in clinical operations.

------

### Slide 20 — Seven People Failure Areas

*Where People Actually Fails*

People failure is the quietest failure domain. There are seven common areas.

First: **stakeholder mapping failure.** The project does not know who matters, where they stand, or what authority they hold.

Second: **critical node exposure.** A person who controls decisions, resources, information, or informal influence sits in resistance.

Third: **the authority-behavior gap.** Someone holds formal accountability but does not exercise it. This is worse than having no owner, because it creates the appearance of control.

Fourth: **champion deficit.** The advocate network does not cover the terrain it needs to cover.

Fifth: **neutral zone inertia.** The undecided population is not moving, even after communication and training.

Sixth: **resistance mismanagement.** Legitimate concern is treated as political opposition, or political opposition is treated as a training problem.

Seventh: **tracking decay and regression.** The stakeholder map was accurate at the start, then the landscape changed and no one updated it.

Five of these seven can fail silently. There is no dashboard for disengagement.

------

### Slide 21 — People: The Cross-Layer Effect

*People Is Not the Third Equal. It Is the Substrate.*

People is not just the third layer. It is the substrate the other two layers depend on.

When the authority-behavior gap is present, process decisions go unmade, inconsistency goes unchallenged, and regression goes unaddressed.

When the neutral zone does not move, adoption fails even if the system is technically sound and training was completed.

When champions burn out, both process compliance and system adoption lose their sustaining mechanism at the same time.

A technically sound system deployed into passive resistance will fail.

A well-designed process owned by nominal champions will regress.

People failure is usually the last to be noticed and the most expensive to recover.

------

## SECTION 6 — SYNTHESIS

------

### Slide 22 — The Three Failure Chains

*How Failure Actually Moves Across the Framework*

Now we put the pieces together.

The first chain is **The Silent Parallel State**.

The current state was never honestly mapped. The future process cannot hold. Staff route around the system. The transition never closes. Data splits. Reports become unreliable. Resistance hardens because staff now have evidence that the system cannot be trusted.

The second chain is **The Post-Launch Collapse**.

Support withdraws after go-live. The system deteriorates gradually. Champions lose credibility and disengage. The process erodes. No one is tracking it, because the monitoring cadence ended when the project team left.

The third chain is **The Authority Vacuum**.

A senior leader holds formal accountability but does not exercise it. Process ownership becomes nominal. The system runs, but the improvement never materializes. Critical decisions are made by default. The system becomes less fit for purpose over time.

Each chain has an entry point. Intervening there is far cheaper than managing the chain after it has already run.

------

### Slide 23 — Implement: The Project Lifecycle

*The Framework Across the Project*

The framework does not change across the project lifecycle. The risk focus changes.

In **Initiation and Discovery**, the risk is building on a false foundation. This is where real workflows, stakeholder positions, infrastructure, data quality, and success criteria are established.

In **Design and Configuration**, the risk is embedding failure into the design. Many process decisions are made inside system configuration before anyone names them as process decisions.

In **Testing and Piloting**, the risk is a clean test environment that does not predict real deployment. This is the last safe point to refine response protocols.

In **Go-Live and Transition**, the risk is treating go-live as the finish line. The priority is closing the transition period quickly, with floor support, leadership presence, unit-level monitoring, and a thirty-day escalation trigger.

In **Post-Live Sustainment**, the risk is failure after the project team leaves. Ownership transfer, twelve-month compliance monitoring, and scheduled stakeholder reassessments are not optional.

Across all phases, the outputs must become live instruments: success criteria, assumption trackers, risk registers, detection cadences, prevention tasks, and response protocols.

If those outputs stay in a slide deck, the framework has not been implemented.

------

### Slide 24 — Closing

*Failure Is the Default. Success Is What You Build.*

We began with the Core Statement. It is worth hearing again.

*"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

The first time, that sentence was a premise. Now it is a summary.

This framework does not promise a successful implementation. It gives you the structure to make failure visible early enough to act.

Before the silent parallel state becomes normal.

Before the post-launch collapse begins.

Before the authority vacuum swallows the improvement the project was meant to deliver.

The work is the discipline. The discipline is the protection.
