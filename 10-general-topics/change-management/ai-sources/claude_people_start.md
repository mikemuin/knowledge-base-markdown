## Initiation

This is an excellent addition — and it belongs in the People layer at a foundational level, not just as a reference tool. Let me stress-test it and show you exactly why, before we build.

------

**What this scale does that the rest of the framework doesn't**

Technology and Process failure can be mapped through systems, workflows, and data. You can observe a broken interface. You can measure a hybrid state. People failure is different — it is *positional*. Where a person sits on this scale determines whether every other layer of the framework can function. A technically perfect system deployed into a field of Passive Resistance will fail. A well-designed process owned by Active Resistors will never reach future state.

This scale gives you the one thing the failure control map logic alone cannot: **a behavioral coordinate system.** It tells you not just *that* someone is a risk, but *where they are* and *what movement looks like*.

------

**Three things the scale gets exactly right**

**The Neutral zone is not safe ground.** *"Waiting to see who wins"* is a precise and important characterization. Neutrality in a change project is not passive — it is a political calculation. The neutral stakeholder is watching whether resistance or support produces better outcomes. If active resistors appear to be winning — diverting funding, creating delays, recruiting others — neutral stakeholders drift downward. If champions are visibly succeeding, neutrals drift upward. This means your management of Active Resistance is not just about the resistor. It is about the signal it sends to everyone in the neutral zone.

**Passive Resistance is your highest silent failure risk.** Loud Anti-Project is visible and therefore manageable — you know who it is and what they are doing. Passive Resistance is covert. Slow-rolling, missed deadlines, being *too busy* — these are behaviors that can be attributed to workload, competing priorities, or personality. This is where the sociotechnical dynamics question becomes essential, because you need a framework for distinguishing genuine capacity constraints from deliberate friction.

**The movement model implies sequence but not certainty.** The scale is presented as a path from bottom to top, which is correct as an aspiration. But movement is not guaranteed to be linear or permanent. People regress. A stakeholder who reaches Passive Support can slide back to Neutral if they perceive the project losing momentum. A champion who loses organizational support can disengage. The scale needs a regression awareness built into it — the same way the Process layer built regression awareness into the future state.

------

**Where it connects to what we've already built**

Your two strategic questions — *"What are the sociotechnical dynamics at play?"* and *"Who needs to decide?"* — now have a structural home. The scale answers both:

- Sociotechnical dynamics are *the forces that determine where people sit on the scale and what moves them*
- Who needs to decide maps directly to *who on the scale has decision authority* — because a decision made by someone in the Passive Resistance zone is not a real decision; it is a delay mechanism

The ownership annotations we introduced in the Process map — the idea that having a title is different from exercising authority — are now fully explained by this scale. A clinical operations lead who is in the Neutral zone owns the domain on paper. They do not own it in practice.

------

**One structural addition I'd propose**

The scale currently runs from Active Resistance to Active Support. I'd suggest adding one dimension that cuts *across* the scale rather than along it: **positional authority**.

Because the danger profile changes completely depending on where on the organizational hierarchy the stakeholder sits:

- An Active Resistor with no authority is a noise problem. Manageable.
- An Active Resistor with budget authority is an existential threat.
- A Passive Resistor at the C-suite level is more dangerous than a Loud Anti-Project at the unit level — precisely because the behavior is invisible and the authority is real.

This means your stakeholder map needs two axes, not one: *where they sit on the scale* and *what they can do from there.* The combination of those two produces your actual risk rating.

------

**How this reshapes the People failure control map**

In Technology, the failure domains came from inverting the success definition. In Process, same logic. In People, the failure domains come from a different structure — because People failure is not about a system state, it is about a behavioral state.

I'd propose the People failure domains be organized around two things this scale makes visible:

1. **Positioning failures** — stakeholders stuck at the wrong level for the role they need to play
2. **Movement failures** — stakeholders who were moving upward and stopped, or regressed

That gives us a map organized around the dynamics of the scale, not just a list of things that can go wrong with people.

## Discussion

That's the right instinct — and it changes the design of the People layer significantly.

Using it as an ongoing tool means the scale is not a snapshot. It is a **dynamic tracking instrument**. Stakeholder positions shift — upward under good management, downward under neglect, pressure, or perceived project failure. The People failure control map has to be built for that dynamism. It cannot be a static risk register.

------

**What ongoing use implies structurally**

If you are tracking position continuously, three things follow:

**You need a cadence.** Stakeholder positions don't change on a schedule, but your assessment of them should. The question is what triggers a reassessment — is it time-based (monthly), event-based (after a key decision, a go-live, a leadership change), or signal-based (a specific behavior that indicates movement)? In practice, the most effective answer is all three: a regular baseline assessment, event-triggered reassessment at key milestones, and signal-based rapid response when behavioral indicators appear.

**Movement is as important as position.** Where someone sits today matters less than the direction and velocity of their movement. A stakeholder currently in Passive Resistance who is moving upward toward Neutral is a different management problem than one who is stationary or drifting downward. Your tracking tool needs to capture trajectory, not just coordinates.

**The map decays without active maintenance.** Stakeholder maps built at project initiation and never updated are worse than useless — they create false confidence. The People layer fails most often not because the initial map was wrong, but because it was accurate at month one and nobody updated it at month four when the landscape shifted.

------

**This also answers a question the scale raises but doesn't answer on its own**

The scale tells you *where* someone is. It doesn't tell you *why* — and the why determines the intervention.

A stakeholder in Passive Resistance could be there because:

- They have a legitimate unaddressed concern about the project
- They feel excluded from decisions that affect their domain
- They are protecting a resource or workflow they depend on
- They are politically aligned with someone who is an Active Resistor
- They simply don't believe the project will succeed — and don't want to back a loser

Each of these requires a completely different response. Misdiagnosing the reason and applying the wrong intervention can push someone further down the scale rather than up. This is where the sociotechnical dynamics question does its real work — it is the diagnostic tool that explains the *why* behind the *where*.

------

**Before we define success — one more structural piece**

Your framing includes champions explicitly. I want to make sure the People map treats champions as a managed asset, not a happy accident.

Champions are almost always treated as a discovery — you find them, you're grateful, you use them. But in a well-designed People framework, **champion cultivation is an active intervention**, not a passive benefit. That means:

- Identifying *potential* champions early — people with credibility, reach, and upward mobility on the scale
- Creating the conditions that move them to Active Support
- Protecting them from organizational pressure that pushes them back
- Distributing them strategically across the stakeholder map — particularly into the Neutral zone, where their influence is highest
- Managing their fatigue, because champions burn out when they are over-relied upon and under-supported

Champions are your primary mechanism for moving the Neutral zone. Not communication plans. Not training programs. Not executive mandates. The person in the Neutral zone who is *waiting to see who wins* is most often moved by a peer they respect who has already decided.

------

**Now we are ready to define success for the People layer.**

And given the dynamic tracking framing, the success definition here will look different from Technology and Process — because success is not a state you reach. It is a **condition you sustain** across a continuously shifting behavioral landscape.

Here is my opening proposition — tell me where it needs to be sharpened:

> *The People layer succeeds when enough stakeholders of sufficient authority are positioned at Passive Support or above — with active champions distributed at critical nodes in the organizational network — to sustain forward project momentum through resistance, absorb setbacks without regression, and progressively move the neutral zone upward as the project matures.*

Three things this definition does deliberately:

**"Enough stakeholders of sufficient authority"** — not all stakeholders, not universal buy-in. That is an unachievable standard and a distraction. The question is whether you have *critical mass* at the right positions in the hierarchy. This is where the two-axis framing (scale position × organizational authority) becomes the operational definition of success.

**"Distributed at critical nodes"** — champions are not interchangeable. A champion in the informatics team does not cover the clinical operations gap. Champion distribution is a strategic placement problem, not a headcount problem.

**"Progressively move the neutral zone upward"** — the neutral zone is your largest addressable population and your most important leverage point. Success in the People layer is measured partly by how much of the neutral zone has moved to Passive Support by go-live, and how much of Passive Support has moved to Active Support by ninety days post-live.
