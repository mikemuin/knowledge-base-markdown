# Health IT Change Management Framework

## Process Layer

**Layer Role:** Execution System
**Framework Logic:** Define → Anticipate → Prevent → Detect → Respond

------

## I. What It Is

> *Process is the Execution System. What you are managing: the transition from what is to what it should be — from how things are done today to how they need to be done tomorrow.*

When a healthcare organisation implements new technology, the goal is never really about the technology itself. The goal is to change *how people work* — how they record information, hand off tasks, coordinate care, and make decisions. That is what the Process Layer manages: the journey from the current way of working to the new one.

The Process Layer has one defining character: **it is the easiest of the three layers to underestimate.**

Every clinical process exists in two versions at the same time:

| Version                  | What It Is                                                   |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **The official process** | What the policy says. What the flowchart shows. What leadership believes is happening. |
| **The real process**     | What staff actually do — shift by shift, under time pressure, with limited resources. |

The gap between those two versions is where new system implementations either succeed or fall apart. In most projects, that gap is never honestly measured before the future state is designed. Organisations end up building the future on a misrepresented picture of the present.

------

## II. Definition of Success

> *The future state workflow is executing as designed — by actual clinical staff, in real operational conditions — with informal workarounds consciously addressed, the transition period compressed to its minimum necessary length, measurable improvement in defined outcomes achieved, and no sliding back to the old way of working.*

Each part of this definition matters. Leaving any one out produces a process that looks successful and isn't.

The most common mistake is defining process success as: *the new workflow is documented, people are trained, and the system is live.* That is a definition of launch, not success. A workflow can be documented, trained, and live — and still be failing. Launch is an event. Success is a sustained condition.

| What Success Requires                          | What It Actually Means                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Executing as designed**                      | The workflow on paper matches the workflow in practice — not just for enthusiastic early adopters, but for all staff, across all shifts and sites, under normal and difficult operating conditions. |
| **By actual clinical staff**                   | The standard is the *average* staff member executing correctly as a matter of routine — not just the trained champions or super-users. A process that only works for the best performers has been demonstrated, not succeeded. The worst-performing unit is where failure lives. |
| **In real operational conditions**             | Short-staffed nights. Surge periods. Competing priorities. System slowdowns. If the process only works when everything is going well, it is not robust enough. It needs to hold up under the worst conditions, not just the ideal ones. |
| **Informal workarounds consciously addressed** | Every workaround and undocumented habit found during planning was deliberately dealt with — either built into the new process, preserved intentionally, or retired because the new process genuinely replaces it. A workaround that was not found is a future problem. |
| **Transition period compressed**               | The period where some staff are using the new way and some are using the old cannot be eliminated — but it must be kept as short as possible. It is the highest-risk period in any implementation. |
| **Measurable improvement**                     | Correct execution without actual improvement is not success. Outcomes must be defined before design begins, baselines established before launch, and results measured at regular intervals afterward. |
| **No regression**                              | The new way of working holds under pressure without the project team actively maintaining it. Old habits do not quietly return when attention shifts elsewhere. |

------

## III. Why It Is the Easiest Layer to Underestimate

Process feels manageable because you can see it. You can map it, diagram it, present it in a meeting. That visible, tidy picture creates a false sense of control — because what gets drawn in a planning room is almost never what actually happens on the floor.

That said, there are four specific places where process is consistently underestimated — and where the problems that emerge are harder to spot than they first appear:

| Where Process Gets Underestimated                      | What Actually Happens                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Unofficial workarounds**                             | Every clinical environment runs on undocumented habits and informal handoffs that staff developed over years to cover gaps in the formal process. These are invisible in planning exercises held in conference rooms. When a new system disrupts them without anyone realising they existed, there is no plan to replace what breaks. |
| **The go-live asymmetry**                              | Technology launches on a specific date. Behavioural change does not. The system goes live, but half the staff are still using the old process. This gap — where the system is live but the process is not yet there — is where most implementations fracture. Organisations treat go-live as the finish line for process. It is where the highest-risk phase begins. |
| **Hidden process decisions**                           | When a system is configured, hundreds of decisions are made along the way — who records what, in what order, with what handoffs, escalated to whom. Most of those decisions are made by IT staff during setup, not by clinical leaders during design. By the time the system goes live, the process has already been decided. The organisation just does not know it yet — and the consequences arrive as operational friction, not technical errors. |
| **Behaviour does not follow technology automatically** | Changing a system does not change behaviour. Behaviour changes gradually, incompletely, and often in reverse. The assumption that process will naturally follow technology — without active management — is the single most consistent underestimation in health IT projects. |

## IV. Critical Insights

### A. The Three States, Not Two

It is common to think of a project as having a start state and an end state. The reality is there are three — and each requires its own planning, monitoring, and management.

| State                | What It Is                                                   | Key Risk                                                     |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Current State**    | How work is done today — including the official process and all the informal workarounds running beneath it | Almost always misrepresented in planning                     |
| **Transition State** | The period where old and new processes are running simultaneously | The highest-risk period of the entire project                |
| **Future State**     | The new way of working, operating stably without active project support | Often designed for ideal conditions that do not exist in practice |

Projects that only design for the start and end states — treating the transition as a logistical detail — consistently produce the worst outcomes. The transition state is not a bridge between the other two. It is a distinct operational condition with its own failure modes and its own management requirements.

### B. Why the Transition Period Becomes Permanent

> **The transition period is the most dangerous operational state in any health IT implementation.**

During this period: some staff are using the new process, some are using the old one, some records are in the system, some are on paper, and no one has a complete picture of what is happening with any given patient. This is when mistakes happen. This is when information gets lost. This is when care coordination breaks down.

The most dangerous thing that can happen to a transition period is that it stops feeling like one. Within approximately thirty days of launch, if the old and new processes are still running side by side, staff stop perceiving the situation as temporary. It becomes the new normal. At that point, it is no longer a transition — it is a failed implementation dressed as a work in progress.

**A controlled step backward is recoverable. A normalised transition period is not.**

### C. What Compresses the Transition Period

You cannot eliminate the transition period. Accepting that reframes the objective: stop trying to prevent it, start actively closing it. The factors that determine how long it lasts are specific and manageable.

| Factor                                | What It Requires                                             |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **A clear cutover plan**              | A defined point at which the old process stops and the new one begins — with explicit, published rules about what documentation lives where during the transition |
| **Concentrated on-the-floor support** | Support staff deployed heavily during the transition window — not spread evenly across the project timeline; this is where the return on support investment is highest |
| **Operational authority**             | Someone with the actual power to close the old process: remove the paper forms, withdraw access to old systems, mandate the cutover — this cannot come from the project team alone |
| **Sustained leadership visibility**   | Active presence from operational leadership throughout the transition — the transition normalises fastest when senior attention withdraws |

## V. Process Failure Control Map

The seven failure areas below each correspond to one element of the success definition. Each one describes what goes wrong, what to watch for, how to prevent it, and who is responsible.

> ⚠ indicates **high risk of silent failure** — situations where things can go seriously wrong without obvious warning signs. Five of the seven areas carry this risk.

### Summary Overview

| Area                                                 | What Goes Wrong                                              | ⚠    | Responsible                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| P1 · Getting the current picture wrong               | New process designed against a false picture of how things actually work today | ⚠    | Clinical Process Owner / Business Analyst         |
| P2 · Designed for Ideal Conditions                   | Workflow works in planning but falls apart in real operating conditions |      | Clinical Informatics Lead / CMIO                  |
| P3 · Stuck Between Old and New                       | Old and new processes run simultaneously until the hybrid becomes the new normal | ⚠    | Project Manager / Clinical Operations Lead        |
| P4 · Missing How Work Actually Gets Done             | Informal practices disrupted by the new process with no plan to replace them | ⚠    | Clinical Process Owner + Front-line Staff         |
| P5 · Process working for champions, not for everyone | Inconsistent execution hidden below headline compliance numbers | ⚠    | Clinical Operations Manager / Unit Leads          |
| P6 · Process works but produces no improvement       | Compliance achieved; outcomes flat                           | ⚠    | CMIO / Project Sponsor                            |
| P7 · Sliding back to the old way of working          | Future state erodes under pressure after the project closes  |      | Clinical Operations Management / Department Heads |

------

### P1 · Getting the Current Picture Wrong

**What goes wrong:** The new process is designed based on a false picture of how things actually work today.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Planning workshops produce the sanitised, official version of how work is done — not the reality · Night shifts, weekend patterns, and surge conditions are excluded from discovery · Unofficial workarounds are never surfaced · Staff are not comfortable being honest about what actually happens when their manager is in the room |
| **How to prevent it**          | Go to where work happens — shadow sessions and direct observation across all shifts, not just conference room workshops · Create a way for staff to share what they actually do without fear of judgment · Explicitly document what is uncertain or contested in the current state picture before design begins |
| **How to spot it early**       | New workarounds emerge after launch that mirror old habits — a signal the old informal processes were never found, only displaced · Staff saying "that's not how we actually do it" after the new process goes live · Data fields left empty that were assumed to be routinely completed |
| **What to do when it happens** | Stop future-state design and re-assess the current picture using better discovery methods · Bring current state discrepancies into the design process as active constraints, not post-design corrections |
| **Who is responsible**         | Clinical Process Owner / Business Analyst — requires access to front-line staff across all shifts and the organisational permission to hear what is actually happening, not the official version |

------

### P2 · Designed for Ideal Conditions

**What goes wrong:** The new workflow looks sound in planning but falls apart when it meets real operating conditions.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Designed for best-case conditions — full staffing, reliable systems, adequate time — none of which are guaranteed · Clinical staff reviewed the design but were not part of creating it · Too many steps for the time and staffing levels available in practice · Process decisions were made inside system configuration without anyone recognising them as process decisions |
| **How to prevent it**          | Involve a representative range of clinical staff as designers, not just reviewers · Test the workflow against worst-case conditions before launch — short-staffed shifts, surge scenarios, system slowdowns · Walk average performers through the workflow, not just champions · Explicitly surface and review every process decision embedded in system configuration |
| **How to spot it early**       | Staff finding workarounds during training or piloting — before the system is even live · Feedback consistently pointing to specific steps as impossible under real conditions · High error or abandonment rates during training simulations |
| **What to do when it happens** | Redesign the workflow with the same clinical involvement required for the original design · Delay launch if the workflow cannot survive stress-testing — difficult, but less damaging than discovering it after go-live · Escalate to the CMIO where redesign requires clinical authority to mandate new practice standards |
| **Who is responsible**         | Clinical Informatics Lead / CMIO — redesigning a clinical workflow requires clinical authority; a project manager cannot mandate it |

------

### P3 · Stuck Between Old and New

**What goes wrong:** The transition period — where old and new processes run simultaneously — never closes. It becomes the new operational normal.

> This is the organising risk of the Process Layer. Every other failure area either contributes to the transition period persisting, or is made worse by it. A normalised transition is not a phase — it is a failed implementation.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | No defined point at which the old process ends — the implementation drifts from old to new without a clear transition · Staff are uncertain which process applies; uncertainty defaults to the familiar, which is the old process · Phased rollouts with no explicit phase closure dates allow each phase to persist indefinitely · Leadership attention withdraws within weeks of launch; the transition normalises in the absence of active oversight |
| **How to prevent it**          | Define a clear cutover point — big bang where operationally feasible, phased only where necessary — with explicit phase-end dates and formal closure protocols · Publish unambiguous rules about what documentation lives where and which process applies to whom during the transition · Concentrate on-the-floor support in the transition window, not spread evenly across the project timeline · Keep leadership visibly present and actively engaged through the full transition period |
| **How to spot it early**       | Both system entries and paper records exist for the same clinical events · Different shifts following different versions of the process · Staff reporting they are not sure which process applies · Data gaps in reports corresponding to activities still being recorded on paper |
| **What to do when it happens** | Deploy additional floor support immediately — within hours, not days · If the transition is entrenched and widespread, implement a controlled, temporary step back to the old process with a defined re-launch plan · Escalate to operational leadership: closing the transition requires authority to physically end the old process |
| **Who is responsible**         | Project Manager / Clinical Operations Lead — requires operational authority to actually close the old process: remove paper forms, revoke old system access, enforce the cutover; this cannot come from the project team alone |

------

### P4 · Missing How Work Actually Gets Done

**What goes wrong:** The undocumented habits and informal handoffs that were quietly holding things together are disrupted by the new process — and no one had a plan to replace them.

> Informal workarounds are not inefficiencies. They are compensating mechanisms for genuine gaps in the formal process. Removing them without replacement does not improve care — it exposes the gap they were covering.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Planning exercises start from official documentation and find only what is already documented · Workarounds identified during discovery are dismissed as bad habits rather than understood as compensating mechanisms · Discovery is conducted at times or in settings that miss shift-specific, site-specific, or surge-specific practices · No deliberate decision is made about what to do with each workaround found |
| **How to prevent it**          | Use direct observation as a core discovery method — not just workshops · Ask explicitly: *what do you do when the formal process does not work?* · For every workaround found, make a deliberate decision: build it into the new process, preserve it intentionally, or retire it with a clear rationale · Involve front-line staff as co-discoverers, not just interview subjects |
| **How to spot it early**       | New workarounds emerging in areas where informal practices were concentrated · Care coordination gaps in the first ninety days in areas where undocumented handoffs were common · Staff describing "gaps" in the new process — which are frequently the gaps left by removed informal practices |
| **What to do when it happens** | Rapid discovery in the affected areas — finding what was missed in the original assessment · Urgent workflow adjustment to address what is missing · If a patient safety function was being performed by an informal process that was removed without replacement, escalate immediately through patient safety channels |
| **Who is responsible**         | Clinical Process Owner + Front-line Staff — requires direct participation of staff as co-discoverers; a manager who represents the floor is not the same as someone who works on it |

------

### P5 · Process Working for Champions, Not for Everyone

**What goes wrong:** The new workflow executes correctly for trained enthusiasts and well-supported units — and inconsistently or not at all for everyone else.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Adoption is measured by aggregate numbers or showcase units — which masks the tail · Training was designed for the ideal learner in ideal conditions and does not transfer to the actual learning environment of a working clinical shift · Compliance is maintained by peer pressure rather than operational management — effective for engaged staff, ineffective for resistant or indifferent ones · "Most people are doing it" is accepted as success when the actual standard is "all people are doing it consistently" |
| **How to prevent it**          | Track compliance by unit, not in aggregate — averages hide the worst-performing areas · Validate training effectiveness with average performers, not champions · Make process compliance an operational management accountability from launch day — not a project team accountability · Define the minimum acceptable compliance threshold and the intervention protocol when a unit falls below it |
| **How to spot it early**       | Unit-level audits showing sharp variation in how the process is followed · Observable differences between shifts and sites during floor rounds · Staff feedback about colleagues not following the process — often the first signal, and frequently dismissed |
| **What to do when it happens** | Targeted retraining at the specific units where compliance is low — not a blanket organisation-wide retraining · Escalate through operational management when retraining alone is insufficient · Investigate whether the root cause is a training gap, a design flaw, or a people issue — the response is different for each |
| **Who is responsible**         | Clinical Operations Manager / Unit Leads — execution inconsistency is an operational management accountability; the project team can detect and escalate, but cannot mandate practice standards |

------

### P6 · Process Works but Produces No Improvement

**What goes wrong:** The workflow is followed as designed — and the improvements the project was meant to deliver do not materialise.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Outcome measures were defined after launch with no baseline to compare against · Measures track whether people followed the steps, not whether anything actually improved · The workflow was designed without an explicit connection to the outcome it was meant to produce · Metrics were chosen based on what the system could easily report, not on what actually matters |
| **How to prevent it**          | Define outcome measures before design begins — not after launch · Establish baselines during current state assessment, before any change is made · Draw an explicit line between each element of the new workflow and the result it is supposed to produce · Distinguish between process compliance metrics (leading indicators) and process outcome metrics (the actual measure of success) |
| **How to spot it early**       | Outcome measures flat or declining at thirty, sixty, and ninety days post-launch despite high reported compliance · Improvement promised at the start of the project not appearing in any operational data |
| **What to do when it happens** | If compliance is high but outcomes are not moving, the design is the problem — not the execution; reopen the workflow design question · Escalate to senior leadership — outcome misalignment is a strategic question about whether the project is delivering its intended value · Revisit scope: if the designed workflow is correct but the outcome is not moving, what was changed may be insufficient |
| **Who is responsible**         | CMIO / Project Sponsor — outcome misalignment requires authority to reopen design decisions and redefine scope; that authority does not live at the project team level |

------

### P7 · Sliding Back to the Old Way of Working

**What goes wrong:** The new way of working gradually erodes and old habits return — often long after the project team has moved on.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | The project team disengages at launch without transferring ownership to the operational management structure · Compliance monitoring stops when the project closes · Operational pressure — surges, staff turnover, competing priorities — displaces new process behaviours in favour of the familiar · No one in the ongoing management structure was ever formally assigned responsibility for sustaining the process |
| **How to prevent it**          | Build the sustainment structure before launch — embedded in the management routines of the units affected, not handed over as an afterthought · Formally transfer process ownership from the project team to operational management before the project closes: documented, deliberate, and complete · Continue compliance monitoring for a minimum of twelve months post-launch · Define escalation pathways: who identifies regression, who escalates, who has authority to respond |
| **How to spot it early**       | Paper forms reappearing at the point of care · Old workarounds coming back in areas that were previously compliant · Staff describing the new process in past tense — *"we used to do it this way"* · Periodic compliance audits at sixty, ninety, one hundred eighty, and three hundred sixty days showing declining trends |
| **What to do when it happens** | Intervene early — the sooner regression is caught, the lower the cost of recovery · Targeted retraining before regression becomes normalised · Process communication refresh to re-establish visibility and accountability · If regression is widespread and entrenched, treat re-implementation as a formal project — not an operational correction |
| **Who is responsible**         | Clinical Operations Management / Department Heads — sustainment ownership must be embedded in the operational management structure from the beginning; it cannot live with the project team because the project ends |

------

### Key Observations Across All Seven Areas

**Most process failures are invisible until they are serious.** Five of the seven areas can go wrong without any obvious warning sign. The process appears to be running while the failure builds quietly underneath. Detection — actively looking for problems through floor rounds, unit-level audits, and outcome measurement — is as important as prevention. A dropped system message can be caught by an automated alert. A gradually normalising transition period or a quietly returning workaround has no equivalent.

**The transition period is where the risk concentrates.** Every failure area either contributes to the transition period persisting or is made worse once it has. Misrepresenting the current state makes the transition harder to close. Poor future state design extends it. Missed workarounds create gaps inside it. Execution inconsistency means it lingers in pockets even when leadership believes it is over. Managing the Process Layer well means, above all else, managing the transition period deliberately.

**Different problems need different decision-makers.** No single person holds the type of authority required to address every failure area. Getting the current picture wrong requires access to front-line staff. Designing a survivable future state requires clinical authority. Closing the transition period requires operational authority. Sustaining the process after launch requires the management structure to own it. A governance structure that cannot activate the right authority at the right moment turns the failure control map into a planning document — not a management tool.

**Launching is not the same as succeeding.** The most consistent failure in Process management is treating launch as the finish line. A workflow that is documented, trained, and live can be failing — and frequently is. The success definition for this layer describes a sustained condition, not a dated event. Active management must extend beyond launch for long enough that the new way of working holds without project team support.

## VI. How Process Failure Spreads to Other Layers

Process problems do not stay within the Process Layer. They trigger failures in the systems staff use (Technology) and in how people engage with the change (People) — and failures in those layers flow back into process in return.

### A. Process Problems That Affect Technology

| What Fails                                               | What It Triggers                                             |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Getting the current picture wrong (P1)**               | If the organisation did not have an accurate picture of how work was actually done before the project started, the requirements used to select and configure the system were built on a false foundation. The system is then deployed into a reality it was never designed for — and the mismatch presents as a technology problem. |
| **Designed for Ideal Conditions (P2)**                   | If the redesigned workflow cannot hold up under real operational pressure — staffing shortfalls, high demand, time constraints — staff will find ways around it. Those workarounds usually involve bypassing the system: keeping paper records, entering data in batches, skipping fields. What looks like a technology adoption failure frequently began as a process design failure. |
| **Stuck Between Old and New (P3)**                       | When the transition persists, data is split between the old process and the new system simultaneously. Reports become unreliable. No one has a complete picture. The system is technically operational but clinically untrustworthy — a technology problem created by a process failure. |
| **Missing How Work Actually Gets Done (P4)**             | When informal workarounds that were performing a data or coordination function are removed without replacement, the gap surfaces as missing information in the system. The system did not lose the data — the process that was generating it was removed. |

### B. Process Problems That Affect People and Engagement

| What Fails                                               | What It Triggers                                             |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Getting the current picture wrong (P1)**               | When staff recognise that the new process does not reflect how care is actually delivered, trust in the design — and in the project leadership — is damaged early. People who might have been broadly supportive become sceptical. Resistance gains a legitimate basis. |
| **Process working for champions, not for everyone (P5)** | When compliance is visibly uneven — some units following the new process, others quietly not — it signals to the broader workforce that the project is not being held to account. The perception that non-compliance has no consequences makes resistance easier to sustain and harder to address. |
| **Sliding back to the old way of working (P7)**          | Staff who actively championed the new process within their teams face a credibility problem when regression becomes visible around them. The effort they invested is publicly undermined. Their willingness and capacity to advocate erodes — at exactly the point when sustained engagement is most needed. |

### C. People and Technology Problems That Feed Back into Process

Failure travels in both directions. The process not only creates problems elsewhere — it also receives them.

| What Fails Elsewhere                                         | How It Affects Process                                       |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Key decision-makers not genuinely committed to the change (People)** | A senior stakeholder who is nominally supportive but not genuinely committed will not exercise the authority needed to close the transition period, address execution inconsistency, or prevent regression. Process failures that require a leadership decision to resolve will persist indefinitely without it. |
| **Staff not moving toward the new way of working (People)**  | Behavioural resistance among staff means the process cannot execute as designed regardless of how well it was built. What presents as execution inconsistency or regression frequently has its root cause in the People Layer — in stakeholders who were never genuinely engaged, supported, or prepared for the change. |
| **Loss of experienced advocates over time (People)**         | The staff who champion the new process in their teams are the primary defence against regression. When those advocates burn out, move on, or disengage, the process loses its most important source of peer-level enforcement and support — and the conditions for regression are in place. |
| **System unreliable or difficult to use (Technology)**       | When the technology the process depends on behaves inconsistently or adds significant burden to the workflow, staff lose confidence in the process itself. Workarounds re-emerge. The transition period extends. What began as a technology problem becomes a process problem — and the two reinforce each other. |
| **Wrong information, real consequences (Technology)**        | When staff cannot trust the information in the system, they develop parallel informal processes to verify or compensate — paper logs, manual checks, unofficial handoffs. The formal process and the informal workaround run simultaneously. The transition period reconstitutes itself from a technology failure. |
