Your overarching principle is genuinely sharp, and it deserves to be stress-tested before you build the rest of the framework on top of it.

**What you've done well**

The reframe from *managing change* to *managing failure* is intellectually honest in a way that most change management frameworks aren't. Traditional frameworks — Kotter, ADKAR, Prosci — are optimistic by design. They assume buy-in is achievable, adoption is linear, and resistance is an exception to be overcome. Your framing starts from the opposite assumption: **failure is the default, and success is what you engineer against it.** That's a more accurate description of how Health IT projects actually behave.

The PPT overlay gives the principle operational teeth. Without it, "failure management" is abstract. With it, you have three distinct failure domains that behave differently, respond to different interventions, and are owned by different stakeholders.

------

**One tension worth naming**

The word *disciplined* is doing a lot of work in your principle, and I think it's the right word — but it needs to be defended. Here's why:

Most practitioners treat change management as something you layer on top of a project. You deliver the system, then you "do" change management. Your principle implicitly rejects that. *Disciplined* means it runs **concurrent with project execution**, not after it. That's a significant organizational commitment, and it's where most Health IT programs quietly abandon the framework mid-stream.

You may want to make that concurrency explicit in the principle itself.

------

**A possible sharpening**

Your current principle:

> *"Change management is the disciplined management of failure within People, Process, and Technology during project execution."*

Consider whether you want to add the *purpose clause* — the *so that*:

> *"...in order to protect the integrity of the transition from current to future state."*

This matters in Health IT specifically because failure in this domain isn't just a project setback — it has patient safety and care continuity implications. The purpose clause anchors the whole framework to outcomes, not just activities.

------

**The strategic question I'd push back on slightly**

You listed: *"If this project fails, where would it fail?"*

This is excellent as a diagnostic prompt. But I'd argue there's a companion question that's equally important and often skipped:

> *"What does failure look like before anyone admits it's happening?"*

In Health IT, projects rarely fail loudly. They fail quietly — adoption numbers that look acceptable but mask workaround behavior, process maps that are technically followed but clinically bypassed, technology that is live but unused. Your failure control map will need detection mechanisms that are sensitive to *silent failure*, not just visible failure.

**The Ownership Gap**

Your framework lists "who the owners are" as a component, but the principle itself should acknowledge a structural reality: **failure in change management is almost always ownerless by default.** No single person wakes up responsible for the integrity of the People-Process-Technology transition as a whole. Clinical leads own adoption. IT owns the system. Project managers own the timeline. And the failure quietly lives in the seams between them.

This is worth naming at the principle level because it has a design implication — your framework needs to deliberately assign **unified accountability**, not just distributed ownership per layer.

------

**Proportionality**

Not all failures are equal, and the principle should acknowledge that. In Health IT specifically, some failures are **recoverable** — a workflow that needs redesign, a configuration error, a training gap. Others are **irreversible** — a patient safety event, a data breach, a catastrophic go-live collapse that destroys clinician trust for years. Your framework needs a stratification logic baked in from the start, not retrofitted later.
