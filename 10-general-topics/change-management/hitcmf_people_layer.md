# Health IT Change Management Framework

## People Layer

**Layer Role:** Behavior System
**Framework Logic:** Define → Anticipate → Prevent → Detect → Respond

------

## I. What It Is

> *People is the Behavior System. What you are managing: stakeholder behavior from start to finish — engagement, ownership, and adoption across the full arc of the project.*

The People Layer is the most human dimension of any Health IT implementation. It is not managing a system, a workflow, or a schedule. It is managing where people stand in relation to the change — and whether they move in the direction the project needs them to move.

Every person touched by a Health IT implementation carries a behavioral position — a posture toward the change. That posture shapes whether they engage, comply, resist, or quietly route around what is being built. The People Layer exists to surface those positions, manage their movement, and make sure the behavioral landscape of the organization is capable of carrying the project forward.

> *The People Layer does not manage communication plans or training calendars. It manages human behavior — which is a different, harder, and more consequential problem.*

------

## II. Definition of Success

> *The People Layer succeeds when enough stakeholders of sufficient authority are positioned at Passive Support or above — with active champions distributed at critical nodes across the organizational network — to sustain forward project momentum through resistance, absorb setbacks without regression, and progressively move the neutral zone upward as the project matures.*

Each part of this definition matters. Leaving any one out produces a project that looks like it has people support and does not.

| What Success Requires                           | What It Actually Means                                       |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Enough stakeholders of sufficient authority** | Not everyone. Not universal buy-in — that is an unachievable standard and a distraction. The question is whether the right people, at the right levels, are genuinely on board. This is where the Two-Axis Framework becomes operational: the right scale positions held by people with the right authority to exercise them. |
| **Distributed at critical nodes**               | Champions are not interchangeable. Having ten enthusiastic advocates in informatics does not cover the gap in clinical operations. Champion coverage is a placement problem, not a headcount problem. Success requires that every critical node type has someone at or moving toward Active Support. |
| **Sustain forward momentum through resistance** | Resistance is not a failure condition — it is a constant. The People Layer has not succeeded when resistance disappears. It has succeeded when the project can absorb resistance and keep moving. This requires a resistance management capability, not just a resistance management plan. |
| **Absorb setbacks without regression**          | Delayed go-lives, failed pilots, and public criticism are tests. A People Layer built on genuine engagement holds through them. One built on surface compliance collapses under the first real pressure — because the stakeholder positions were performative, not real. |
| **Progressively move the neutral zone upward**  | The large middle of the stakeholder population is neither resisting nor advocating. The direction and speed at which this group moves toward support is the leading indicator of People Layer health. The neutral zone is the largest addressable population in any implementation, and moving it is the highest-leverage activity in this layer. |

------

## III. Why It Is the Most Challenging Layer

Technology failure is visible. A system goes down. An interface breaks. A report produces wrong numbers. Someone notices, an alert fires, and a response begins.

Process failure is observable. A workflow is not being followed. Documentation is split between paper and digital. A handoff is missed. These breakdowns leave evidence.

**People failure is neither visible nor observable. It accumulates in silence.**

A stakeholder who was supportive at the start of the project and has quietly drifted to disengaged by month four generates no alert. A champion who is burning out withdraws from advocacy before anyone notices the withdrawal. A senior leader who approves everything in a governance meeting and slow-rolls everything in operation is, by every formal measure, a supporter — and by every operational measure, an obstacle.

By the time People failure becomes visible, it has usually been present long enough to have already damaged the Process and Technology layers it was supposed to support.

| Where People Gets Underestimated                  | What Actually Happens                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **The silence of behavioral failure**             | Unlike a system outage or a missed process step, a stakeholder drifting from support to resistance generates no alert. There is no monitoring dashboard for disengagement. Detection requires deliberate human observation against a maintained tracking instrument — a discipline that is easy to abandon under project pressure. |
| **The reach of People failure into other layers** | Every process in the Process Layer has a human being executing it. Every system in the Technology Layer has a human being adopting or ignoring it. When the People Layer fails, it does not fail in isolation — it pulls the other two layers down with it, often invisibly, often long before anyone calls it a failure. |
| **The absence of a finish line**                  | Technology has a go-live date. Process has a future state target. People has neither. Behavioral commitment, champion engagement, and neutral zone movement do not resolve on a project timeline. The People Layer must be managed actively after go-live — not wound down with the project structure. |

------

## IV. Critical Insights

### A. People Failure Does Not Announce Itself

The most common reason People failure goes unmanaged is that it looks like something else until it is too late. A stakeholder who was mapped as supportive and has drifted is not raising concerns — they are simply less present, less responsive, less active. A champion who is burning out is not resigning — they are just contributing less. A neutral zone that is not moving is not resisting — it is waiting.

By the time any of these conditions becomes formally visible, it has usually been accumulating for months. The project team that is not actively looking for People failure will not find it until it has already damaged the Process and Technology layers that depend on it.

This is why detection in the People Layer is not a passive function. It requires a maintained tracking instrument, a defined assessment cadence, and the discipline to reassess continuously — not just at project initiation.

### B. The Neutral Zone Is the Highest-Leverage Target

In nearly every Health IT implementation, the largest single population of stakeholders is neither actively resisting nor actively supporting. They are waiting — watching the project's momentum, observing the behavior of people they respect, and reading the organizational signals around them.

This population — the neutral zone — is the primary leverage point in the entire People Layer. Moving even a portion of it toward Passive Support has more impact on project momentum than converting a single Active Resistor. And the primary mechanism for moving it is peer influence — not communication plans, not training programs, not executive mandates.

The person in the neutral zone who is waiting to see who wins is most reliably moved by a peer they trust and respect who has already decided. That is the fundamental logic of the champion network — and why champion placement, not champion headcount, is the strategic priority.

### C. People and Process Cannot Be Managed Separately

The Process Layer describes what work should be done and how. The People Layer determines whether anyone actually does it. A well-designed process owned by a nominal champion will regress. A clearly documented workflow ignored by the people responsible for following it is not a future state — it is a document.

Process ownership is a People problem wearing a Process label. Execution inconsistency is partly a function of whether staff trust the process enough to follow it. Process regression is partly a function of whether leaders are still attending to it. The two layers cannot be read in isolation — and their failure modes cannot be managed as if they belong to separate teams.

## V. Failure Control Map

Seven failure domains organized around two structural failure types: **positioning failures** — stakeholders stuck at the wrong scale position for the role they need to play — and **movement failures** — stakeholders who were moving upward and stopped, regressed, or were never tracked.

> ⚠ indicates **high silent failure risk** — failure most likely to be invisible before it becomes irreversible or normalized.

### Summary Overview

| Domain                              | What Fails                                                   | ⚠    | Owner                                  |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---- | -------------------------------------- |
| Pe1 · Flying Blind          | The project team does not know who is where, why, or what authority they hold | ⚠    | Change Manager + Project Sponsor       |
| Pe2 · Influential Opponent  | A decision, resource, influence, workflow, or network node sits below Passive Support with no active management of the gap | ⚠    | Project Sponsor / Executive Leadership |
| Pe3 · Owner in Name Only    | A stakeholder holds formal authority but does not exercise it | ⚠    | Project Sponsor                        |
| Pe4 · Not Enough Advocates  | The champion network does not cover the organizational terrain it needs to cover | ⚠    | Change Manager + Executive             |
| Pe5 · Too Many Fence-Sitters | The largest addressable population is not moving toward support |      | Champion Network                       |
| Pe6 · Resistance Mismanagement | Resistance is handled incorrectly — escalated when it should be engaged, or generalized when it requires individual diagnosis |      | Change Manager                         |
| Pe7 · Stale Situation       | The stakeholder map was accurate at initiation and never updated; the behavioral landscape shifted unseen | ⚠    | Change Manager                         |

------

### Pe1 · Flying Blind

**What fails:** The project team does not know who is where, why they are there, or what authority they hold. Decisions are being made without a map.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Stakeholder identification limited to formal governance rosters — the register captures who attends steering committees and misses the influence node three levels down whose opinion moves the neutral zone · Scale positions assigned by assumption rather than observed behavior — seniority treated as a proxy for support, silence treated as agreement · The *why* behind each position never diagnosed, making it impossible to design an intervention · Stakeholder map built at project initiation and treated as settled for the remainder of the project |
| **How to prevent it**          | Structured discovery that goes beyond the org chart — explicitly ask: *"Who has blocked projects like this before? Who do people listen to when they are uncertain? Who controls the resources this project depends on?"* · Base every scale position on observed behavior, not role or assumption · Document the reason behind every position below Passive Support at project initiation · Build the cadence for updating the map before the project begins, not after the first surprise |
| **How to spot it early**       | Discrepancies between what the map says and what is actually happening in steering committees and operational interactions · Decisions delayed or qualified in ways the map did not predict · Surprises — resistance from someone mapped as supportive, or unexpected disengagement — are signals the map is wrong, not isolated incidents |
| **What to do when it happens** | Immediate reassessment of the full stakeholder landscape, not just the person who surprised the team · One wrong position usually means others are wrong too — treat it as a map problem, not a stakeholder problem · Re-diagnose with behavioral evidence; close the gap between the map and observable reality before proceeding |
| **Who is responsible**         | Change Manager + Project Sponsor                             |

------

### Pe2 · Influential Opponent

**What fails:** A decision, resource, influence, workflow, or network node sits below Passive Support with no active management of that gap.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Decision nodes appear supportive in formal settings and do not follow through in operational ones — the slow-roll that never becomes refusal · Resource nodes in Passive Resistance whose behavior is interpreted as organizational busyness rather than a behavioral position · Influence nodes in Active Resistance broadcasting skepticism to a neutral zone that is watching · Critical nodes identified at project initiation but not reassessed when their positions shift |
| **How to prevent it**          | Map every critical node against both axes — scale position and organizational authority — at project initiation · Do not accept a title as evidence of a position; validate through behavior · Define what each critical node type needs to look like to be operationally safe, and manage to that standard continuously |
| **How to spot it early**       | Patterns of delay, redirection, or non-follow-through at specific points in the project timeline · Decisions that are approved in governance and not executed in operations — the gap between what is said and what is done · Neutral zone movement stalling in areas where a critical influence or network node is in an unmanaged resistant position |
| **What to do when it happens** | Peer-level authority engagement — the right person to address a critical node is usually someone at their own organizational level · The Change Manager alone cannot close this gap; it requires the Project Sponsor or executive leadership to engage directly · Do not attempt to manage a high-authority resistant critical node through communication tactics |
| **Who is responsible**         | Project Sponsor / Executive Leadership                       |

------

### Pe3 · Owner in Name Only

**What fails:** A stakeholder holds formal authority — an ownership title, a governance role, an accountability assignment — but does not exercise it. They are nominally responsible and behaviorally absent.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Process ownership titles assigned without securing a behavioral commitment — the owner approves things in meetings and does not follow up in operations · Governance accountability that exists on paper and is never enforced · Leaders who do not model the behavior they are nominally responsible for promoting · The gap between having a title and exercising authority treated as a personality issue rather than a structural failure |
| **How to prevent it**          | Distinguish between assigning a title and securing a behavioral commitment — they are different conversations · Define what exercising authority looks like specifically and observably before the role is assigned · Build a check into the project cadence that confirms owners are actively exercising their authority, not just holding the title |
| **How to spot it early**       | Process decisions that never get made despite a named owner · Escalations that reach the owner and go nowhere · A pattern where the person with the title is consistently absent from the moments that require the title to be exercised |
| **What to do when it happens** | Direct executive engagement with the stakeholder — this cannot be managed by the Change Manager alone · The conversation must happen at or above the authority level of the stakeholder · If the gap cannot be closed, escalate immediately — a nominal owner is more dangerous than no owner, because it creates the appearance of accountability without the substance |
| **Who is responsible**         | Project Sponsor                                              |

------

### Pe4 · Not Enough Advocates

**What fails:** The champion network does not cover the organizational terrain it needs to cover — because champions are absent from critical nodes, concentrated where support already exists, or burning out under unsustainable load.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Champions recruited wherever willing rather than placed where needed — coverage reflects availability, not strategic requirement · No coverage at key critical node types, creating dead zones in the neutral zone movement strategy · Champion fatigue developing undetected — advocacy frequency declining, responses becoming shorter, withdrawal from voluntary activities beginning before anyone notices · Organizational pressure on champions that the project team is not seeing and not addressing · Champion health tracked as a headcount rather than as an engagement level |
| **How to prevent it**          | Design the champion network against the critical node map — placement first, recruitment second · Monitor champion health at every tracking cadence, separately from general stakeholder tracking · Define what a healthy champion looks like behaviorally — frequency of advocacy, peer reach, organizational protection — and track against it · Manage champion load actively; reduce the ask before fatigue sets in, not after withdrawal begins |
| **How to spot it early**       | Areas of the organization where the neutral zone is not moving despite communication and training activity · Champion engagement declining in observable behavioral signals — less frequent, less energetic, less visible advocacy · Coverage gaps appearing as critical nodes change or champions disengage |
| **What to do when it happens** | Targeted recruitment for the specific gap — not a general call for champions, but a deliberate approach to a named node or population · Load redistribution before champions withdraw, not after · Executive re-engagement to provide visible organizational protection when champions are under pressure; public backing from leadership is the most effective protection a champion can receive |
| **Who is responsible**         | Change Manager + Executive                                   |

------

### Pe5 · Too Many Fence-Sitters

**What fails:** The largest addressable population is not moving. The "waiting to see who wins" calculation is not resolving in the project's favor.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Formal communication reaching the neutral zone but carrying no peer credibility — it reads as project-sponsored messaging, not a genuine signal from someone who has made a real choice · The wrong intervention applied — executive mandates and training programs address knowledge and obligation, not the social uncertainty that keeps people neutral · A stalled neutral zone misread as passive compliance rather than behavioral suspension · Champion absence or disengagement in the specific area where the neutral zone is concentrated |
| **How to prevent it**          | Build peer influence channels into the engagement strategy from the start · Place champions at network nodes specifically to reach the neutral zone through informal pathways that formal communications cannot access · Do not rely on communication plans to do what only peer credibility can do — design for peer signal from the beginning |
| **How to spot it early**       | The neutral zone not thinning — the same population sitting in the same position at successive tracking intervals · Areas of the organization where communication and training have reached people but their position has not changed · The absence of movement is the signal; it requires no drama to be serious |
| **What to do when it happens** | Diagnose which peer signals the neutral zone is receiving and from whom · Redirect champion energy toward the specific concentration of neutrals that is not moving · Address the specific concerns circulating in informal channels — not the generic concerns assumed by the project team · If the neutral zone is not moving in a specific area, a champion coverage gap is the most likely cause; diagnose before escalating |
| **Who is responsible**         | Champion Network                                             |

------

### Pe6 · Resistance Mismanagement

**What fails:** Resistance is handled incorrectly — escalated when it should be engaged, dismissed when it should be diagnosed, or treated as uniform when it requires individual attention.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | All resistance treated as political opposition — a stakeholder whose legitimate concern was never addressed is misread as an opponent · Escalation applied before understanding — converting a recoverable position into an entrenched one by responding to the symptom rather than the cause · Active Resistance and Passive Resistance conflated — the same intervention applied to both despite the fact that they require fundamentally different responses · Resistance mismanaged at the individual level and then used as a broadcast by the resistor to the neutral zone watching around them |
| **How to prevent it**          | Diagnose before intervening — every resistor sits where they sit for a reason; understanding that reason determines whether the right response is engagement, accommodation, escalation, or sustained pressure · Distinguish between Active and Passive Resistance as a precondition to any intervention · Build resistance diagnostic logic into the Change Manager's methodology at project initiation |
| **How to spot it early**       | Resistance hardening rather than softening after an intervention — a sign that the wrong approach was applied · A resistor communicating more openly and more critically with the neutral zone after a project team engagement — using the interaction as material for skepticism · Interventions that are consuming project team time without producing position movement |
| **What to do when it happens** | Re-diagnose the position and the driver before reapplying any intervention · Match the response to the actual position on the scale and the actual source of resistance — not the position assumed or the source presumed · Accept that some resistance cannot be converted and focus energy on containing its influence on the neutral zone rather than eliminating the position itself |
| **Who is responsible**         | Change Manager                                               |

------

### Pe7 · Stale Situation

**What fails:** The stakeholder map was accurate at project initiation and was never updated. The behavioral landscape shifted. The project team did not see it. The map says the project is safe — and it is not.

|                                |                                                              |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **How this happens**           | Assessment cadence abandoned after initiation — the kickoff map was thorough and useful; months later it has not been revisited · Event triggers not defined — the team reassesses on a schedule but does not know to reassess when a leadership change occurs, a go-live is delayed, or a champion disengages · Champion regression undetected — a champion at Active Support in month two is at Passive Support by month five; the project team still counts them as Active Support; the coverage map is wrong · Post-go-live tracking abandoned — stakeholder management treated as a pre-go-live activity; after go-live, the tracking stops; regression begins and no one is watching |
| **How to prevent it**          | Define the tracking cadence before the project begins across three simultaneous rhythms: *time-based* (regular baseline review, monthly minimum during active implementation), *event-triggered* (immediate reassessment at defined events: leadership changes, milestone delays, go-live events, public resistance incidents, champion disengagement), and *signal-based* (rapid response when behavioral indicators appear that predict position change before it is confirmed) · Maintain champion-specific tracking separately from general stakeholder tracking · Build post-go-live reassessment into the sustainment plan as a non-negotiable commitment — ninety-day and six-month reassessments at minimum |
| **How to spot it early**       | The delta between the current map and the previous assessment — not where people are, but where they moved to and in which direction; the direction of movement is the signal · Behavioral leading indicators that predict position change before the change is confirmed: a champion becoming less responsive, a supportive stakeholder beginning to vocalize concerns, a neutral zone that was thinning beginning to stabilize |
| **What to do when it happens** | Act on downward movement immediately — the cost of intervention rises steeply as regression deepens; a stakeholder who has moved from Passive Support to Neutral can often be re-engaged with a single meaningful conversation; a stakeholder who has reached Active Resistance requires a sustained campaign · Update the map as the first output of any response action; the map must reflect current reality at all times · If widespread regression is detected post-go-live, treat it as a formal re-engagement effort with defined milestones, accountability, and executive sponsorship — not a routine operational correction |
| **Who is responsible**         | Change Manager — sustained through project closure and into operational sustainment |

## VI. How People Layer Failure Spreads to Other Layers

People problems do not stay within the People Layer. When the behavioral landscape fails, it triggers failures in how work gets done (Process) and how tools are used (Technology) — and failures in those layers can flow back into People in return.

### A. People Failures That Affect How Work Gets Done

| What Fails                              | What It Triggers                                             |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Owner in Name Only (Pe3)**        | A process owner who is nominally accountable but behaviorally absent means process decisions go unmade, execution inconsistency goes unchallenged, and regression goes unaddressed. Every process domain that requires an owner to exercise authority is exposed when that authority is withheld. |
| **Resistance Mismanagement (Pe6)**  | When the intervention applied to a resistor confirms their belief that the project is not listening, they become more committed to behaviors that preserve the current state — and they communicate that commitment to the neutral zone around them. Resistance that was individual becomes a process norm. |
| **Stale Situation (Pe7)**           | When the stakeholder map is not maintained, the process ownership and compliance monitoring that depends on People ownership is the first thing to erode. Process regression and People tracking decay run on the same timeline, in the same direction, invisibly. |
| **Not Enough Advocates (Pe4)**      | The champion network is the primary mechanism for sustaining process compliance in the months after go-live. When champions burn out or coverage gaps emerge, the peer reinforcement that holds the future state in place disappears. The process begins to drift back toward familiar patterns. |

### B. People Failures That Affect How Technology Gets Used

| What Fails                       | What It Triggers                                             |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Influential Opponent (Pe2)**     | A governance member in Passive Resistance who controls vendor approval does not block selection — they shape it toward the path of least resistance, which is frequently not the path of best clinical fit. Technology selection failure begins in the People Layer. |
| **Too Many Fence-Sitters (Pe5)**   | A system can be technically sound, properly configured, and adequately trained — and fail to achieve adoption because the People Layer never moved the neutral zone to a position where using the system was the natural behavior. Technology adoption failure is often a People failure wearing a Technology label. |
| **Not Enough Advocates (Pe4)**     | The champion network is the primary sustaining mechanism for system adoption after launch. When champions burn out or are unevenly distributed, the peer reinforcement that sustains the system disappears. Post-launch deterioration follows — not because the system changed, but because the human infrastructure that supported it did. |

### C. Process and Technology Failures That Feed Back into People

Failure travels in both directions. The People Layer does not only create problems elsewhere — it also receives them.

| What Fails Elsewhere                                      | How It Affects People                                        |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **System that staff cannot use efficiently (Technology)** | When the system adds friction to clinical work — more steps, slower navigation, poor fit with real workflows — staff experience it as a broken promise. People who were Passive Support before go-live move toward Neutral. Champions who staked their credibility on advocating for the system face a trust problem with their peers. |
| **System degrading after launch (Technology)**            | Individuals who championed the system and brought colleagues along with them lose credibility when the system deteriorates. Their capacity and willingness to advocate erodes at exactly the moment when it is most needed. The champion network loses its most invested members. |
| **Process regression (Process)**                          | As the future state erodes, the behavioral landscape that supported it erodes with it. Champions who sustained the process lose their organizational rationale for advocacy. Stakeholders who had moved to Passive Support read the regression as a signal that the project did not succeed — and begin to drift downward. |
| **Current state never accurately understood (Process)**   | If the organization did not have an accurate picture of how work was actually done before the project began, the future state designed to replace it was built on a false foundation. When staff recognize that the new process does not fit their real working conditions, their resistance is not unreasonable — it is a rational response to being told to follow something that was never designed for how they actually work. |

## Key Cross-Cutting Observations

### The Ownership Pattern

The consistent pattern across all seven People domains: the **Change Manager owns the methodology**; the **Project Sponsor owns the authority interventions**. When one operates without the other, the People Layer has structural gaps that no amount of tactical skill can close.

| Domain                   | What Is Required                    | Who                                    |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Flying Blind             | Diagnostic skill + executive access | Change Manager + Project Sponsor       |
| Influential Opponent     | Peer-level authority                | Executive Leadership / Project Sponsor |
| Owner in Name Only       | Hierarchical authority              | Project Sponsor                        |
| Not Enough Advocates     | Network design + executive backing  | Change Manager + Executive             |
| Too Many Fence-Sitters   | Peer influence                      | Champion Network                       |
| Resistance Mismanagement | Behavioral diagnostic skill         | Change Manager                         |
| Stale Situation          | Sustained discipline + mandate      | Change Manager                         |

### The Silent Failure Concentration

Five of seven People domains carry silent failure risk — the highest concentration across all three layers of the framework. People failure is structurally the most silent dimension of any project. A system outage can be detected by a monitoring alert. A stakeholder drifting from support to disengagement generates none. Detection in the People Layer requires deliberate human observation and a maintained tracking discipline. It cannot be automated, and it cannot be delegated to a communication calendar.

### People Does Not End at Go-Live

The Technology Layer has a go-live date. The Process Layer has a future state target. The People Layer has neither. Behavioral commitment, champion engagement, and neutral zone movement do not resolve on a project timeline. The stakeholder landscape continues to shift after go-live. Champions burn out. Leadership changes alter the political environment. Staff turnover replaces trained advocates with untrained newcomers who inherit the informal behavioral norms of whoever trained them.

The failure control map must be read as a **sustained management discipline**, not a project-phase deliverable. The tracking cadence, the champion network, and the resistance management capability do not end at go-live — they transition from the project team to the operational management structure in exactly the same way that process ownership must be formally transferred before the project closes.

### The Relationship Between People and the Other Layers

People does not sit beside Process and Technology as just another equal layer. It is what makes the other two work. A technically sound system deployed into a field of Passive Resistance will fail. A well-designed process owned by nominal champions will regress. The People Layer is the condition that makes the other two layers possible — and its failure is the last to be noticed and the most expensive to recover.
