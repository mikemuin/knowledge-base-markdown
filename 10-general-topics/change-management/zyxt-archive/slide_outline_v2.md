# Slide Outline — Failure-Aware Change Management Framework

**Target audience:** Non-technical professionals (conference) **Target time:** 20 minutes (~24 slides) **Section structure:** Introduction · How To Use · Technology · Process · People · Synthesis **Version:** 2 — revised for impact

------

## SECTION 1 — INTRODUCTION

*5 slides | ~4 minutes*

------

### Slide 1 — Title

**Title:** Why Health IT Projects Fail — And What To Do About It **Subtitle:** The Failure-Aware Change Management Framework **Notes:** Field-tested, not theoretical. Establish that immediately.

------

### Slide 2 — The Problem

**Title:** The Pattern That Keeps Repeating **Content:** Three statements — each a real project post-mortem:

- "The system went live on time — but staff never really adopted it."
- "The process looked right on paper — but that's not how anyone actually worked."
- "Leadership was supportive — until they weren't."

**Subtext:** These are not isolated failures. They are the same failure, wearing different clothes. **Notes:** Open with recognition. Every person in the room has lived one of these. Don't rush this slide — let it land before moving on.

------

### Slide 3 — The Central Premise

**Title:** This Framework Starts From the Opposite Assumption **Content:** Two-column contrast:

- **Traditional frameworks:** Adoption is achievable. Resistance is the exception to be managed.
- **This framework:** Failure is the default. Success is what you deliberately engineer against it.

**Subtext:** That is not pessimism. It is the discipline that traditional optimism consistently fails to supply. **Notes:** ← This is the reframe that earns the room's attention. Don't soften it. The provocation is the point.

------

### Slide 4 — The Silent Failure Problem

**Title:** 14 of 20 Ways This Can Fail Generate No Alert **Content:**

- In most project environments, failure is visible: a missed deadline shows on the plan, a budget overrun shows in the report, a system outage fires an alert.
- In Health IT change management, the most consequential failures do not announce themselves. They accumulate — in the gap between what the stakeholder map says and what is actually happening, in the parallel process no one is tracking, in the data flowing incorrectly between systems that appear to be working.
- Detection cannot be passive. It must be actively designed — with instruments, cadences, and named owners whose specific job is to look for things that are not announcing themselves.

**Subtext:** The question most commonly skipped in project planning: *"How will we know if something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?"* It is skipped because it is uncomfortable — it requires looking for evidence that the project is not working, at the moment when organizational pressure demands you demonstrate that it is. **Notes:** ← **NEW SLIDE.** This is the most important conceptual addition to the outline. The 14-of-20 number is the single most arresting fact in the framework. Give it a slide of its own before introducing anything else.

------

### Slide 5 — The Framework at a Glance

**Title:** One Core Statement. Five Governing Principles. Three Layers. **Content:** Two elements:

**The Core Statement:**

> *"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

**The three layers:**

- **People** — the Behavior System
- **Process** — the Execution System
- **Technology** — the Enablement System

**Notes:** Brief orientation before How To Use. The Core Statement appears here for the first time. It will reappear at the close. Both appearances matter.

------

## SECTION 2 — HOW TO USE THE FRAMEWORK

*4 slides | ~3 minutes*

------

### Slide 6 — The Three-Step Loop

**Title:** Identify. Question. Implement. **Content:**

- **Identify** — take inventory of what belongs to each layer: who is involved, what workflows are changing, which systems are being deployed
- **Question** — run six structured questions against each item identified
- **Implement** — embed the answers into the live project as a running discipline, not a planning document

**Subtext:** This is not a one-time exercise. It runs from the first planning meeting through go-live and beyond. **Notes:** The loop framing is intentional — it signals continuous iteration, not a completion event.

------

### Slide 7 — The Six Questions

**Title:** The Same Six Questions. Applied to Everything. **Content:** Two columns of three:

- What does success look like?
- What are we taking for granted?
- Where could we fail?
- **How will we know if something is going wrong?** ← most commonly skipped
- What do we do now to reduce the chance of failure?
- If something fails, what do we do?

**Subtext:** Question 4 is the one that requires active design — a cadence, a method, and a named owner. Without it, detection is an intention, not a discipline. **Notes:** Call out Question 4 explicitly and connect it back to Slide 4. The silent failure problem is why that question exists.

------

### Slide 8 — Who Does This Work

**Title:** Two Types of Ownership. Both Are Required. **Content:** Two-column contrast:

- **Methodology ownership — Change Manager** Designs and maintains the approach. Runs the diagnostic instruments. Manages the detection cadence. Identifies problems and escalates them. *Cannot close problems that require organizational authority.*
- **Authority ownership — Project Sponsor / Executive Leadership** Has the power to act on what the methodology surfaces: close a transition period, challenge a vendor, mandate a practice standard, remove a nominal owner who is not performing. *Does not manage the methodology — creates the conditions under which it can work.*

**Subtext:** When one operates without the other, the framework has structural gaps that no amount of tactical skill can close. A Change Manager without executive backing cannot close a permanent transition period. A Project Sponsor without a functioning methodology has no reliable signal that these problems exist. **Notes:** ← **NEW SLIDE.** This replaces the generic "turning answers into action" slide from v1. The methodology/authority distinction is one of the framework's sharpest structural insights and it was entirely absent from the previous outline.

------

### Slide 9 — The Framework Does Not End at Go-Live

**Title:** The Project Ends. The Framework Doesn't. **Content:** Two-column contrast:

- **What ends at go-live:** the project structure, the implementation team, the launch milestones
- **What must continue:** detection cadence, assumption tracking, stakeholder monitoring, process compliance checks, system health reviews — for a minimum of 12 months

**Subtext:** The framework ends when accountability has been formally transferred to operations — documented, deliberate, and complete. Until that transfer is confirmed, the project is not done. It is in an unmanaged transition. **Notes:** Plant this early. It will be reinforced in each layer section and echoed directly in Synthesis.

------

## SECTION 3 — TECHNOLOGY LAYER

*4 slides | ~3.5 minutes*

------

### Slide 10 — Technology: What We're Managing

**Title:** Technology — The Enablement System **Content:**

- **What it is:** the most visible layer — the system with a name, a vendor, a launch date, and a price tag
- **The visibility trap:** the fact that you can point to the system does not mean the system is working
- **The real success test:** the right system, reliably accessible, easy enough to use that people choose to use it, measurably improving the work it was meant to support — and that remains true long after launch

**Notes:** The "you can point to it" framing cuts through the common assumption that a running system is a successful one.

------

### Slide 11 — The Go-Live Trap

**Title:** Organisations Declare Victory at Precisely the Wrong Moment **Content:** Timeline visual:

- **Launch** → support withdrawn → small problems accumulate → workarounds normalize → quiet deterioration → **visible failure** (months later)

**Subtext:** Adoption is the real measure — not whether the system *can* be used, but whether it *is* being used as intended, consistently, across all staff and all conditions. A system that staff work around has failed, regardless of how well it was built. **Notes:** This is the most important single point in the Technology section for a non-technical audience. The timeline visual makes the failure mechanism concrete.

------

### Slide 12 — Six Technology Failure Areas

**Title:** Where Technology Actually Fails **Content:** Table — one row per failure area, ⚠ marked:

- **T1** · Wrong system selected
- **T2** · Access and infrastructure failures
- **T3** · Data accuracy and system connections ← highest patient safety risk
- **T4** · Staff not using the system
- **T5** · System does not improve the work
- **T6** · Decline after launch

**Subtext:** 4 of 6 can fail without any obvious warning sign. T3 — data accuracy — is the most dangerous: errors here can affect patient safety, are often invisible until someone acts on wrong information, and are frequently misdiagnosed as user error. **Notes:** Call out T3 explicitly. It's the one failure area in the Technology Layer that connects directly to patient harm — and it's also one of the most silent.

------

### Slide 13 — Technology: The Cross-Layer Effect

**Title:** Technology Problems Don't Stay in Technology **Content:** Three cascades, each two lines:

- Data accuracy failure → staff develop informal workarounds → process becomes inconsistent across the organisation
- Staff not using the system → work splits between system and paper → no one has a complete picture of any patient
- Wrong system selected without staff input → trust is damaged early → people who might have been supportive become resistant

**Subtext:** Some of the most significant risks in the Technology Layer are not technical in origin — they come from the People and Process layers. Treating them purely as technical problems produces technical solutions that miss the actual cause. **Notes:** Bridge into the Process section.

------

## SECTION 4 — PROCESS LAYER

*4 slides | ~3.5 minutes*

------

### Slide 14 — Process: What We're Managing

**Title:** Process — The Execution System **Content:**

- **What it is:** the transition from how things are done today to how they need to be done tomorrow
- **The hidden complexity:** every clinical environment runs on two versions of every process simultaneously — the official one (what the policy says, what the flowchart shows) and the real one (what staff actually do under time pressure, with limited resources)
- **What success actually requires:** the new workflow executing correctly, by all staff — not just champions — in real conditions, without regression

**Notes:** The "two versions" framing is the most useful thing to land here. It explains why conference room planning exercises so consistently miss the point.

------

### Slide 15 — The Transition Period

**Title:** The Most Dangerous Operational State in Any Implementation **Content:**

- **What it is:** the interval when old and new processes run simultaneously — some records in the system, some on paper, no one with a complete picture of any given patient
- **Why it is dangerous:** mistakes happen, information gets lost, care coordination breaks down — not because of technical failure, but because two versions of reality are running at once
- **The 30-day rule:** within approximately 30 days of launch, if both processes are still running, staff stop perceiving the situation as temporary. It becomes the new normal.
- **The critical distinction:** a controlled step backward is recoverable. A normalised transition period is not — it is a failed implementation wearing the clothes of a work in progress.

**Notes:** ← The 30-day window is specific, surprising, and actionable. This is the most important concept in the entire Process section. Spend time here.

------

### Slide 16 — Seven Process Failure Areas

**Title:** Where Process Actually Fails **Content:** Table — one row per failure area, ⚠ marked:

- **P1** · Getting the current picture wrong
- **P2** · Designing a process that cannot survive reality
- **P3** · Transition period becoming permanent ← the organising risk
- **P4** · Missing the unofficial workarounds
- **P5** · Process working for champions, not for everyone
- **P6** · Process works but produces no improvement
- **P7** · Sliding back to the old way of working

**Subtext:** 5 of 7 can fail without any obvious warning sign. P4 — missing unofficial workarounds — is the most reliably missed item in any planning exercise. Informal workarounds are not inefficiencies. They are compensating mechanisms for genuine gaps in the formal process. Removing them without replacement does not improve care — it exposes the gap they were covering. **Notes:** The P4 note is new and important. It reframes workarounds for an audience that typically wants to eliminate them.

------

### Slide 17 — Process: The Cross-Layer Effect

**Title:** Process Problems Don't Stay in Process **Content:** Three cascades:

- Inaccurate current picture → system configured against a false foundation → technology misfit surfaces at deployment as a "technology problem"
- Transition period normalises → data splits between old process and new system → system is technically operational but clinically untrustworthy
- Process regression visible → champions who carried the change lose credibility with peers → people layer begins to erode

**Subtext:** Different problems need different decision-makers. Surfacing the real current state requires front-line access. Closing the transition requires operational authority. Sustaining the process after launch requires the management structure to own it. A governance structure that cannot activate the right authority at the right moment turns the failure control map into a planning document — not a management tool. **Notes:** Bridge into the People section. The final subtext line earns its place — it's a precise and uncomfortable observation.

------

## SECTION 5 — PEOPLE LAYER

*4 slides | ~3.5 minutes*

------

### Slide 18 — People: What We're Managing

**Title:** People — The Behavior System **Content:**

- **What it is:** where every person touched by the project stands in relation to the change — and whether they move in the direction the project needs
- **What makes it different from the other two layers:**
  - Technology failure is visible. A system goes down — someone notices, an alert fires.
  - Process failure is observable. A workflow is not being followed — documentation gaps appear.
  - People failure is neither. It accumulates in silence.
- **The longest tail:** Technology has a go-live date. Process has a future state target. People has neither.

**Notes:** The three-way contrast is the most important framing in this section. Let it land before moving on.

------

### Slide 19 — The Neutral Zone

**Title:** The Largest Addressable Population Is Waiting **Content:**

- In nearly every Health IT implementation, the largest single population is neither actively resisting nor actively supporting — they are watching: the project's momentum, the behavior of people they respect, and the organizational signals around them.
- What moves them: not communication plans, not training programs, not executive mandates — but peer influence from someone they trust who has already decided.
- The implication: champion *placement* matters more than champion *headcount*. Having ten enthusiastic advocates in informatics does not cover the gap in clinical operations.

**Subtext:** Moving even a portion of the neutral zone toward passive support has more impact on project momentum than converting a single active resistor. This is the highest-leverage activity in the entire People Layer. **Notes:** This reframe — placement over headcount — is the most actionable insight in the People section for a leader who owns a champion network.

------

### Slide 20 — Seven People Failure Areas

**Title:** Where People Actually Fails **Content:** Table — one row per failure area, ⚠ marked:

- **Pe1** · Stakeholder mapping failure
- **Pe2** · Critical node exposure
- **Pe3** · Authority-behavior gap ← more dangerous than no owner at all
- **Pe4** · Champion deficit
- **Pe5** · Neutral zone inertia
- **Pe6** · Resistance mismanagement
- **Pe7** · Tracking decay and regression

**Subtext:** 5 of 7 can fail without any obvious warning sign — the highest silent failure concentration across all three layers. Pe3 — the authority-behavior gap — deserves special attention: a stakeholder who holds a title and does not exercise it is *more dangerous* than no owner at all, because it creates the appearance of accountability without the substance. **Notes:** The Pe3 callout is new and important. It's counterintuitive and will register with anyone who has managed governance structures.

------

### Slide 21 — People: The Cross-Layer Effect

**Title:** People Is Not the Third Equal. It Is the Substrate. **Content:** Three cascades:

- Authority-behavior gap → process decisions go unmade, execution inconsistency goes unchallenged, regression goes unaddressed — every process domain requiring authority to act is exposed
- Neutral zone not moving → technically sound system, properly trained staff → adoption fails because the behavioral conditions for adoption were never created
- Champion burnout → peer reinforcement disappears → both process compliance and system adoption deteriorate simultaneously, without any obvious triggering event

**Subtext:** A technically sound system deployed into a field of passive resistance will fail. A well-designed process owned by nominal champions will regress. People is the condition that makes the other two layers possible — and its failure is the last to be noticed and the most expensive to recover. **Notes:** This is the closing argument of the three-layer sequence. The final sentence is worth reading aloud.

------

## SECTION 6 — SYNTHESIS

*3 slides | ~2.5 minutes*

------

### Slide 22 — The Three Failure Chains

**Title:** How Failure Actually Moves Across the Framework **Content:** Three named chains — each a compact narrative:

**Chain 1 — The Silent Parallel State** P1 → P2 → T4 → P3 → T3 → Pe6 *The current state was never honestly mapped. The future state process cannot hold. Staff route around the system. The transition never closes. Data splits. Reports become unreliable. Visible failure legitimises the resistance that was there from the start.*

**Chain 2 — The Post-Launch Collapse** T6 → Pe4 → P7 → Pe7 *Support withdraws after go-live. The system deteriorates. Champions disengage. The process erodes. No one is tracking any of it — the monitoring cadence was wound down when the project team left.*

**Chain 3 — The Authority Vacuum** Pe3 → P6 → Pe2 → T1 (compounded) *A senior leader holds formal accountability but does not exercise it. The system runs; the improvements do not materialise. Critical decisions are made by default. The system becomes progressively less fit for purpose.*

**Notes:** ← **NEW SLIDE.** These three chains are the most story-shaped, memorable content in the entire framework and they were entirely absent from v1. A conference audience will remember a named failure chain long after they have forgotten a table of failure domains.

------

### Slide 23 — Implement: The Project Lifecycle

**Title:** The Framework Across the Project **Content:** Five-phase timeline, each with its primary risk:

- **Phase 1 — Initiation & Discovery** → Primary risk: building on a false foundation
- **Phase 2 — Design & Configuration** → Primary risk: embedding failure into the design
- **Phase 3 — Testing & Piloting** → Primary risk: a clean test environment that does not predict real deployment
- **Phase 4 — Go-Live & Transition** → Primary risk: treating go-live as the finish line
- **Phase 5 — Post-Live Sustainment** → Primary risk: failure that accumulates after the project team leaves

**Subtext:** The framework's emphasis shifts across phases — not because different things matter at different times, but because the nature of the risk changes. The instruments most relevant to managing it change with it. **Notes:** ← **NEW SLIDE.** This replaces the previous "Transfer Test" slide, which was too narrow to close a Synthesis section. The lifecycle view gives the audience a practical map for *when* to apply what they have just learned — which is the question a conference audience is most likely to leave asking.

------

### Slide 24 — Closing

**Title:** Failure Is the Default. Success Is What You Build. **Content:**

> *"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

**Subtext:** The framework does not promise a successful implementation. It gives you the structure to make failure visible early enough to do something about it. **Notes:** Return to the Core Statement. Close on the premise, not on optimism. The repetition is intentional — the audience first encountered this sentence on Slide 5 without the context to fully understand it. They have that context now. Questions welcome.
