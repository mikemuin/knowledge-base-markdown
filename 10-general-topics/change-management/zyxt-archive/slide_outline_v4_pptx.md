# Why Health IT Projects Fail — And What To Do About It

- **The Failure-Aware Change Management Framework**
- Field-tested in live hospital implementations
- *Not a theory from a conference room*

<div class="notes">Welcome everyone. This framework comes from field experience: Health IT projects that succeeded, projects that failed, and the more dangerous category — projects that failed quietly enough that no one could agree they had failed at all. Over the next twenty minutes, we will work through a structured approach to change management that starts from a very different premise than most frameworks you have encountered. This is about what happens inside hospitals when systems, workflows, and clinical behavior all have to change at the same time.</div>

## The Pattern That Keeps Repeating

- *"The system went live on time — but staff never really adopted it."*
- *"The process looked right on paper — but that's not how anyone actually worked."*
- *"Leadership was supportive — until they weren't."*
- These are **not** isolated failures
- They are the **same failure**, wearing different clothes

<div class="notes">Open with recognition — every person in this room has lived at least one of these three situations. Don't rush this slide; let each statement land before moving to the next. These three post-mortem quotes represent different symptom presentations of a single underlying problem: change management approaches built to manage success, not to detect the failure that is already forming beneath the surface. Pause after reading the three bullets and invite the audience to consider silently which one resonates most with their own experience. The goal here is identification, not analysis — we will get to analysis shortly.</div>

## This Framework Starts From the Opposite Assumption

:::::::::::::: columns
::: column
- **Traditional frameworks**
    - Adoption is achievable if the right steps are followed
    - Resistance is the exception to manage
    - Success is the expected outcome
    - Optimism is built into the structure
:::
::: column
- **This framework**
    - **Failure is the default**
    - **Success is engineered**
    - Resistance is a signal, not an obstacle
    - Detection is designed, not assumed
:::
::::::::::::::

<div class="notes">This is the reframe that earns the room's attention — do not soften it. The provocation is the point. The statement "failure is the default" sounds pessimistic; it is actually the correction to a form of optimism that has been consistently expensive in clinical environments. The clinical environment is complex, busy, political, and unforgiving. Things do not go smoothly by default — they go smoothly when someone designs the conditions for them to go smoothly. The word "engineered" is deliberate: it means detected, owned, prevented where possible, and contained when prevention fails. That is very different from hoped for.</div>

## 14 of 20 Ways This Can Fail Generate No Alert

- In most project environments, failure is **visible:** missed deadlines, budget overruns, system outages
- In Health IT, the most consequential failures **do not announce themselves:**
    - A stakeholder who supported in month one drifts to disengagement by month four
    - A clinical champion quietly withdrawing — no report captures it
    - Data flowing incorrectly between systems that *appear* to be working
- Detection cannot be passive — it must be **actively designed:**
    - Instruments, cadences, and named owners whose specific job is to look
- The most commonly skipped question: *"How will we know if something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?"*

<div class="notes">This is the most important conceptual slide in the framework. The 14-of-20 number is the single most arresting statistic here — use it. The reason the detection question gets skipped is that it is deeply uncomfortable: it requires looking for evidence that the project is not working at precisely the moment when the organization is demanding proof that it is. Name that tension explicitly. The rest of this framework is largely an answer to that single question — everything that follows is a method for making the silent visible. Pause here before moving on.</div>

## Core Statement. Three Layers.

- *"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*
- Two phrases to hold:
    - **"Concurrent with project execution"** — change management starts on day one, not after go-live
    - **"Integrity of the transition"** — the real goal is a stable operating state *after* launch, not launch itself
- Three operational layers: **People · Process · Technology**

<div class="notes">Read the Core Statement aloud, slowly. Tell the audience they will hear it again at the close, and that it will mean something different the second time. "Concurrent with project execution" corrects the most common implementation mistake: treating change management as a phase that begins after configuration, by which point the most important decisions have already been made. "Integrity of the transition" resets the finish line from launch day to sustained operation. The three layers are not silos — they are three surfaces of the same transition, and failure in one reliably propagates to the others.</div>

## The Five Governing Principles

- **Start on day one, not day done** — change management runs from the first planning meeting
- **Engineer for failure, not optimism** — failure is the default; success is engineered through detection, ownership, prevention, and response
- **What you can't see will hurt you most** — silent failure requires active detection, not passive reporting
- **If everyone owns it, no one owns it** — accountability for the transition must be deliberately assigned
- **Not all failure costs the same** — response must be calibrated to consequence, especially when patient safety is exposed

<div class="notes">These five principles are not platitudes — each one is a correction to a specific, recurring mistake in Health IT implementations. The fifth principle is worth pausing on for this audience: a training gap is recoverable, but a patient safety event is not, and the response to each type of failure must match its consequence. Think of these as the guardrails that prevent the framework from becoming just another checklist with better branding. Ask the audience: which of these principles is your organization currently weakest on? That is usually the most useful self-assessment question in this section.</div>

## Identify. Question. Implement.

- **Identify** — take inventory of everything in each layer:
    - Who is involved, what workflows are changing, which systems are in scope
- **Question** — run six structured questions against each item identified
- **Implement** — embed answers into the live project as a running discipline:
    - Checkpoints, trackers, detection schedules, prevention tasks, response protocols
- This loop is **not a one-time exercise**
- It repeats at every major milestone, through go-live, and into sustainment

<div class="notes">The loop framing is intentional — it signals continuous iteration, not a completion event. Stress the word "implement": it means creating live instruments that are actively used, not documents that are filed. If the outputs of this exercise stay in a slide deck, the framework has not been implemented. Give an example of the difference: a risk register reviewed weekly by a named owner is a live instrument; the same register published at project kick-off and never reopened is a document. The methodology lives in the frequency and discipline of the loop, not in any single artifact it produces.</div>

## The Same Six Questions. Applied to Everything.

- What does **success** look like? *(specific enough that two people can agree it happened)*
- What are we **taking for granted**? *(unconfirmed assumptions are risks with nicer names)*
- Where could we **fail**? *(sorted by consequence: low, high, irreversible)*
- **How will we know if something is going wrong?** ← *most commonly skipped*
- What do we do **now** to reduce the chance of failure? *(prevention requires an owner and a deadline)*
- If something fails, **what do we do**? *(trigger, steps, owner, and recovery target)*

<div class="notes">Call out Question 4 explicitly and connect it back to the silent failure slide. This is the question that requires active design — a cadence, a method, and a named owner. Without it, detection is an intention, not a discipline. For Question 6, stress that for patient safety risks the first step is always containment: stop the harm first, understand it second. The six questions as a pattern are: define success, expose assumptions, identify failure, detect it early, prevent what you can, and prepare for what still breaks. These six questions applied at every major milestone is the simplest possible implementation of this framework.</div>

## Two Types of Ownership. Both Are Required.

:::::::::::::: columns
::: column
- **Change Manager**
    - Owns the methodology
    - Runs diagnostic instruments
    - Manages detection cadence
    - Identifies and escalates problems
    - *Cannot close problems requiring organizational authority*
:::
::: column
- **Project Sponsor / Executive Leadership**
    - Acts on what the methodology surfaces
    - Closes transition periods
    - Mandates practice standards
    - Removes nominal owners not performing
    - *Does not manage the methodology*
:::
::::::::::::::

<div class="notes">This distinction is one of the framework's sharpest structural insights. A Change Manager without executive backing can see the permanent transition period forming and have no power to close it. A Project Sponsor without a functioning methodology has no reliable signal that the transition has become permanent. Neither substitutes for the other, and when one is absent, the failure they would have caught goes unaddressed. Ask the audience: in your last implementation, was both types of ownership clearly assigned and actively exercised? The more common failure is not absence — it is nominal assignment without active exercise.</div>

## The Project Ends. The Framework Doesn't.

:::::::::::::: columns
::: column
- **What ends at go-live**
    - Project structure
    - Implementation team
    - Launch milestones
    - Intense pre-launch activity
:::
::: column
- **What must continue (minimum 12 months)**
    - Detection cadence
    - Assumption tracking
    - Stakeholder monitoring
    - Process compliance checks
    - System health reviews
:::
::::::::::::::

<div class="notes">Plant this concept early — it will be reinforced in each layer section. The framework ends only when two things are true: ownership has been formally transferred to operations, and the receiving teams have the tools, knowledge, and authority to keep running what they have inherited. Not when the transfer is announced — when it is verified. This is a non-obvious and often uncomfortable point for organizations eager to close out a project. The word to stress is "verified" — a handover meeting is not verification; demonstrated capability in real conditions is verification.</div>

## Technology — The Enablement System

- The most **visible** layer: a system with a name, a vendor, a launch date, and a price tag
- **The visibility trap:** a system that exists is not the same as a system that works
- **Real success means:**
    - The right system, reliably accessible
    - Easy enough that staff *choose* to use it
    - Measurably improving the work it was meant to support
    - *And that remains true long after launch day*

<div class="notes">The visibility trap framing is the key concept on this slide. In most organizations, the Technology Layer receives the most attention and the most resources — and yet it is also the layer most prone to premature victory declarations. A system that is running is not the same as a system that is being used correctly, consistently, and safely. The real success test is adoption, not launch. Open this section by noting that organizations often declare technology success on exactly the wrong day — which is precisely the subject of the next slide.</div>

---

## Organisations Declare Victory at Precisely the Wrong Moment

- **Launch** → support withdraws → small problems accumulate → workarounds normalize
- → quiet deterioration begins → **visible failure** months later, building since week one after launch
- **The real measure:** adoption — not whether the system *can* be used, but whether it *is* being used as intended, consistently, across all staff and conditions
- **A system that staff work around has failed** — regardless of how well it was built or how smoothly it launched

<div class="notes">This is the most important single point in the Technology section for a non-technical audience. The mechanism is predictable: support withdraws, small frictions go unaddressed, staff find workarounds, workarounds become habits, and what eventually surfaces as visible failure has been building since the week after launch. The go-live celebration is often the moment the real work stops — and that is exactly when it should be intensifying. Let this one land before moving to the failure areas table on the next slide.</div>

## Where Technology Actually Fails

| Code | Failure Area | Silent? |
|:-----|:-------------|:-------:|
| T1 | Wrong system selected | ⚠ |
| T2 | Access and infrastructure failures | |
| T3 | Data accuracy and system connections ← *highest patient safety risk* | ⚠ |
| T4 | Staff not using the system | ⚠ |
| T5 | System does not improve the work | ⚠ |
| T6 | Decline after launch | |

<div class="notes">Tell the audience not to try to memorize the codes — listen for the pattern: the system can be present, available, and still clinically unreliable. T3 deserves special attention: data accuracy errors can directly affect patient safety, are often invisible until someone acts on wrong information, and are frequently misdiagnosed as user error. This is the most dangerous failure area in the Technology Layer and it operates in near-total silence. The headline to carry forward is that 4 of 6 technology failure areas can fail without any obvious warning sign — uptime monitoring and help desk tickets are insufficient detection mechanisms.</div>

## Technology Problems Don't Stay in Technology

- **Data accuracy failure** → staff develop informal workarounds → process becomes inconsistent across the organisation
- **Staff not using the system** → work splits between system and paper → no one has a complete picture of any patient
- **Wrong system selected without staff input** → trust damaged early → potential supporters become resistant before the hardest phase begins
- If a technology problem keeps returning after a technical fix, the cause **may not be technical**
- Look first in **Process** or **People**

<div class="notes">This cross-layer slide bridges into the Process section. The three cascades demonstrate that technology problems are rarely contained to the technology domain — when data accuracy fails, it creates a process problem; when staff stop using the system, it creates a people problem. The diagnostic principle is: identify the layer of origin, not the layer of presentation. A recurring technology problem that does not respond to technical fixes is usually rooted in process design or people behavior. Ask the audience: when has a "technology problem" in your experience turned out to be something else entirely?</div>

## Process — The Execution System

- **What it is:** the transition from how things are done *today* to how they need to be done *after the change*
- Every clinical environment runs on **two versions** of every process simultaneously:
    - **Official version:** what the policy says, what the flowchart shows
    - **Real version:** what staff actually do under time pressure, with limited resources
- **The gap between those two versions** is where implementations fall apart
- Process success requires the future-state workflow executing correctly, by *all* staff, in real conditions, without regression

<div class="notes">The "two versions" framing is the most useful concept on this slide. It explains why conference room planning exercises so consistently miss the point — they are designed against the official version of the process, not the real one. When the new system goes live, it encounters the real version. The mismatch typically surfaces as a technology problem, but it began as a process failure. Stress that process success is not a signed-off workflow — it is the future-state workflow being executed correctly by actual clinical staff in real conditions, measured, and sustained.</div>

---

## The Most Dangerous Operational State in Any Implementation

- **The transition period:** the interval when old and new processes run simultaneously
    - Some records in the new system, some on paper — no one has a complete picture of any patient
- **Why it is dangerous:** not because the system crashed, but because *two versions of reality are running at once*
- **The 30-day rule:** if both processes are still running after 30 days, staff stop perceiving the situation as temporary — it becomes the new normal
- **The critical distinction:** a controlled step backward is recoverable; a *normalised* transition period is not

<div class="notes">The 30-day window is specific, surprising, and actionable — use it. This is the most important concept in the entire Process section, so spend time here. The transition period is not a sign that the project is struggling — it is a designed, expected operational state. The danger is when it stops being temporary. Once staff normalize dual processes, the path back to a clean future state requires an entirely different intervention. Say this aloud: a normalized transition period is a failed implementation wearing the clothes of a work in progress. That phrase usually registers immediately with anyone who has managed a live implementation.</div>

## Where Process Actually Fails

| Code | Failure Area | Silent? |
|:-----|:-------------|:-------:|
| P1 | Getting the current picture wrong | ⚠ |
| P2 | Designing a process that cannot survive reality | |
| P3 | Transition period becoming permanent ← *the organising risk* | ⚠ |
| P4 | Missing the unofficial workarounds | ⚠ |
| P5 | Process working for champions, not for everyone | ⚠ |
| P6 | Process works but produces no improvement | ⚠ |
| P7 | Sliding back to the old way of working | |

<div class="notes">Five of seven process failure areas can fail silently — the spreadsheet will not tell the whole truth. P4 is worth a special note that is counterintuitive for most audiences: informal workarounds are not merely inefficiencies to be eliminated. They are often compensating mechanisms for genuine gaps in the formal process. If you remove a workaround without understanding what it compensates for, you do not improve care — you expose the gap it was covering. P5 is also commonly missed: aggregate compliance metrics can hide the weak unit, the night shift, or the reluctant department. Floor reality has to be checked directly.</div>

## Process Problems Don't Stay in Process

- **Inaccurate current picture** → system configured against a false foundation → technology misfit surfaces at deployment as a *"technology problem"*
- **Transition period normalises** → data splits between old process and new system → system is technically operational but **clinically untrustworthy**
- **Process regression** → champions who carried the change lose credibility → People Layer begins to erode
- Governance must be able to activate **the right authority at the right moment**
- If it cannot, the failure control map is **paperwork**, not a management tool

<div class="notes">This slide bridges into the People section and introduces the governance observation. The three cascades show that process failures propagate laterally across all other layers — a process problem not addressed becomes a technology problem and then a people problem within a surprisingly short time. The governance observation is precise and uncomfortable: a governance structure that cannot activate the right authority at the right moment turns the failure control map into a planning document. Surfacing the real current state requires front-line access; closing the transition requires operational authority; each needs a named owner with the actual power to act.</div>

## People — The Behavior System

- **What it is:** where each person stands in relation to the change — and whether they are moving in the direction the project needs
- **What makes it different from the other two layers:**
    - *Technology failure* is visible — a system goes down, an alert fires
    - *Process failure* is observable — documentation gaps, missed handoffs
    - *People failure* is **neither** — it accumulates in silence
- **The longest tail:** Technology has a go-live date. Process has a future-state target. **People has neither.**

<div class="notes">The three-way contrast is the most important framing in this section — let it land before moving on. People behavior does not resolve on a project timeline. A stakeholder who was supportive in month one may drift into disengagement by month four, and by formal governance measures may still appear supportive. The only way to detect this is active monitoring — which is exactly why the People Layer has the highest concentration of silent failure modes across all three layers. Note that this is also the layer most commonly addressed with communication plans and training programs, neither of which is sufficient on its own.</div>

---

## The Largest Addressable Population Is Waiting

- In most Health IT implementations, the largest group is **neither actively resisting nor actively supporting**
- They are **watching:**
    - Project momentum
    - The behavior of people they respect
    - Whether leadership is serious after the first pressure point
- What moves them: **not** communication plans, training, or executive mandates — but *peer influence from someone they trust who has already decided*
- **Champion placement matters more than champion headcount**

<div class="notes">This reframe — placement over headcount — is the most actionable insight in the People section for a leader who owns a champion network. Ten enthusiastic advocates in informatics do not cover a gap in clinical operations. Moving even a portion of the neutral zone toward passive support has more impact on project momentum than converting a single active resistor. Ask the audience: in your last implementation, where were your champions placed — in units where resistance was highest, or where buy-in was already assured? The neutral zone is asking a social question: who has already decided, and do I trust that person's judgment?</div>

| Code | Failure Area | Silent? |
|:-----|:-------------|:-------:|
| Pe1 | Stakeholder mapping failure | ⚠ |
| Pe2 | Critical node exposure | ⚠ |
| Pe3 | Authority-behavior gap ← *more dangerous than no owner at all* | ⚠ |
| Pe4 | Champion deficit | ⚠ |
| Pe5 | Neutral zone inertia | |
| Pe6 | Resistance mismanagement | |
| Pe7 | Tracking decay and regression | ⚠ |

<div class="notes">Five of seven people failure areas can fail silently — the highest concentration across all three layers. Pe3 deserves special attention and is counterintuitive: a stakeholder who holds a title and does not exercise it is more dangerous than having no owner at all, because it creates the appearance of accountability without the substance. Pe7 is also worth noting: the stakeholder map was accurate at the start of the project, then the landscape changed and no one updated it. The map no longer reflects reality, but the project is making decisions as if it does. There is no dashboard for disengagement — active monitoring is the only answer.</div>

## People Is Not the Third Equal. It Is the Substrate.

- **Authority-behavior gap** → process decisions go unmade → inconsistency goes unchallenged → regression goes unaddressed
- **Neutral zone not moving** → technically sound system, properly trained staff → adoption fails because *behavioral conditions for adoption were never created*
- **Champion burnout** → peer reinforcement disappears → both process compliance and system adoption deteriorate simultaneously
- A technically sound system deployed into **passive resistance** will fail
- A well-designed process owned by **nominal champions** will regress
- People failure is usually the **last to be noticed** and the **most expensive to recover**

<div class="notes">This is the closing argument of the three-layer sequence — the final three bullets are worth reading aloud and pausing after each one. People is not simply the third equal layer; it is the substrate that makes the other two layers possible. Technology and process can look stable on a dashboard while the behavioral conditions for their success are actively eroding. The failure, when it comes, will look like a technology or process problem — but by the time it surfaces, the People Layer will have been failing for months. Last noticed, most expensive to recover: that is the summary of everything that makes this layer the most difficult and the most consequential.</div>

## How Failure Actually Moves Across the Framework

- **Chain 1 — The Silent Parallel State**
    - Current state never honestly mapped → future process cannot hold → staff route around → transition never closes → data splits → resistance hardens
- **Chain 2 — The Post-Launch Collapse**
    - Support withdraws at go-live → system deteriorates → champions disengage → process erodes → monitoring cadence ended when project team left
- **Chain 3 — The Authority Vacuum**
    - Formal accountability not exercised → improvements never materialise → critical decisions made by default → system becomes less fit for purpose over time
- Each chain has an **entry point** — intervening there is far cheaper than managing the chain after it has run

<div class="notes">These three chains are the most story-shaped, memorable content in the entire framework — a conference audience will remember a named failure chain long after they have forgotten a table of failure domains. Read each chain name, then walk through the narrative at a deliberate pace. The entry point observation is critical: every chain starts with a single identifiable failure mode, and intervening at that point is dramatically cheaper than managing the consequences downstream. Ask the audience: which of these three chains is most recognizable from your own experience? That question usually generates the most engaged conversation in the entire session, because everyone has lived at least one of them.</div>

## The Framework Across the Project

| Phase | Primary Risk |
|:------|:-------------|
| **1 — Initiation & Discovery** | Building on a false foundation |
| **2 — Design & Configuration** | Embedding failure into the design |
| **3 — Testing & Piloting** | A clean test environment that does not predict real deployment |
| **4 — Go-Live & Transition** | Treating go-live as the finish line |
| **5 — Post-Live Sustainment** | Failure that accumulates after the project team leaves |

<div class="notes">This lifecycle view gives the audience a practical map for when to apply what they have just learned. The framework's emphasis shifts across phases — not because different things matter at different times, but because the nature of the risk changes with each phase. In Initiation, the risk is assumptions embedded in the foundation; in Design, decisions embedded in configuration before anyone names them as process decisions; in Testing, false confidence from a controlled environment; at Go-Live, premature closure; in Sustainment, the monitoring cadence ending when the project team leaves. Ask the audience: at which phase does your organization typically begin applying change management disciplines? The answer is almost always too late.</div>

## Key Takeaways

- **Failure is the default** — clinical environments are complex, busy, political, and unforgiving
- **Silent failure is the primary threat** — 14 of 20 failure modes generate no alert
- **Detection must be active and designed** — cadences, instruments, named owners
- **All three layers are interdependent** — failure in one surfaces in another
- **People is the substrate** — behavioral conditions must be created, not assumed
- **The framework does not end at go-live** — minimum 12 months of sustained monitoring
- **Success is engineered** — through detection, ownership, prevention, and response

<div class="notes">Use this slide to consolidate the major themes before the closing. Each bullet corresponds to a major section of the presentation. Ask the audience which takeaway is most relevant to their current work — this usually generates good discussion and helps personalize the material. If the audience takes only one thing away, the most consequential is the second bullet: 14 of 20 failure modes generate no alert. That single fact changes how an organization should think about monitoring. If they take two things, add the last bullet: success is engineered. Those two ideas together are the core of this framework.</div>
