# Slide Outline — Failure-Aware Change Management Framework

**Target audience:** Health IT professionals and hospital implementation leaders (conference)

**Target time:** 20 minutes (~22 slides)

**Section structure:** Introduction · How To Use · Technology · Process · People · Synthesis

**Version:** 6

------

## SECTION 1 — INTRODUCTION

*6 slides | ~5 minutes*

------

### Slide 1 — Title

**Title:** Why Health IT Projects Fail — And What To Do About It

**Subtitle:** The Failure-Aware Change Management Framework

**Notes:** Field-tested, not theoretical. Establish that immediately.

------

### Slide 2 — The Problem

**Title:** The Pattern That Keeps Repeating

**Content:** Three statements — each a real project post-mortem:

- "The system went live on time — but staff never really adopted it."
- "The process looked right on paper — but that's not how anyone actually worked."
- "Leadership was supportive — until they weren't."

**Subtext:** These are not isolated failures. They are the same failure, wearing different clothes.

**Notes:** Open with recognition. Every person in the room has lived one of these. Don't rush this slide — let it land before moving on.

------

### Slide 3 — The Central Premise

**Title:** This Framework Starts From the Opposite Assumption

**Content:** Two-column contrast:

- **Traditional frameworks:** Adoption is achievable. Resistance is the exception to be managed.
- **This framework:** Failure is the default. Success is engineered.

**Subtext:** That is not pessimism. It is the discipline that traditional optimism consistently fails to supply.

**Notes:** ← This is the reframe that earns the room's attention. Don't soften it. The provocation is the point.

------

### Slide 4 — The Silent Failure Problem

**Title:** The Failures That Make No Noise

**Content:**

- In most project environments, failure is visible: a missed deadline shows on the plan, a budget overrun shows in the report, and a system outage fires an alert.
- In Health IT change management, the most consequential failures do not announce themselves. A stakeholder who was supportive in month one drifts into disengagement by month four — no alert fires. A clinical champion starts to withdraw — no report captures it. Data begins flowing incorrectly between two systems that both appear to be working — no dashboard turns red.
- Detection cannot be passive. It must be actively designed — with instruments, cadences, and named owners whose specific job is to look for things that are not announcing themselves.

**Subtext:** The question most commonly skipped in project planning: *"How will we know if something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?"* It is skipped because it is uncomfortable — it requires looking for evidence that the project is not working at a time when organizational pressure demands you demonstrate that it is.

**Notes:** The three examples are concrete enough to be recognizable. Let them land before moving on.

------

### Slide 5 — The Framework at a Glance

**Title:** The Core Statement

**Content:**

The framework begins with one sentence:

> *"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology, from first decision to sustained adoption, moving the organization from current state to future state."*

**Subtext:** This is not a softer project-management slogan. It is an operating definition. The work is to surface failure early, assign ownership, and keep control until adoption is real, because in Health IT the movement from current state to future state is where clinical continuity, trust, and data integrity are most exposed.

**Notes:** Let the sentence breathe. This is the first time the audience sees the Core Statement, so do not bury it under the rest of the framework. The next slide explains what prevents that sentence from becoming decorative language.

------

### Slide 6 — The Framework at a Glance

**Title:** Governing Principles. Three Layers.

**Content:** Two elements:

**The five Governing Principles:**

- **Start on day one, not day done** — change management starts with the first real decision, not after delivery
- **Engineer for failure, not optimism** — failure is the default; success is engineered through detection, ownership, prevention, and response
- **What you can't see will hurt you most** — silent failure requires active detection, not passive reporting
- **If everyone owns it, no one owns it** — unified accountability for the transition must be deliberately assigned
- **Not all failure costs the same** — response must be calibrated to consequence, especially when patient safety or clinician trust is exposed

**The three layers:**

- **People** — the Behavior System
- **Process** — the Execution System
- **Technology** — the Enablement System

**Subtext:** The Governing Principles keep the framework from becoming a checklist with better branding. The layers are not silos; they are three parts of the same change.

**Notes:** Keep the principle explanations tight. This is the orientation slide for how the rest of the deck will move: principles first, then People, Process, and Technology as linked failure surfaces.

------

## SECTION 2 — HOW TO USE THE FRAMEWORK

*5 slides | ~4 minutes*

------

### Slide 7 — The Three-Step Loop

**Title:** Identify. Question. Implement.

**Content:**

- **Identify** — take inventory of what belongs to each layer: who is involved, what workflows are changing, which systems are being deployed
- **Question** — run six structured questions against each item identified
- **Implement** — embed the answers into the live project as a running discipline, not a planning document

**Subtext:** This is not a one-time exercise. It runs from the first decision through sustained adoption.

**Notes:** The loop framing is intentional — it signals continuous iteration, not a completion event.

------

### Slide 8 — The Six Questions

**Title:** The Same Six Questions. Applied to Everything.

**Content:** Two columns of three:

- **Define Success** — What does success look like?
- **Test Assumptions** — What do we think is true?
- **Name Failure Points** — Where could we fail?
- **Design Detection** — **How will we know if something is going wrong?** ← most commonly skipped
- **Reduce Risk Early** — What do we do now to reduce the chance of failure?
- **Prepare Response** — If something fails, what do we do?

**Subtext:** Question 4 is the one that requires active design — a cadence, a method, and a named owner. Without it, detection is an intention, not a discipline.

**Notes:** Call out Question 4 explicitly and connect it back to Slide 4. The silent failure problem is why that question exists.

------

### Slide 9 — Sample Scenario 1: Radiology Access

**Title:** ED Stroke CT, Run Through the Loop

**Content:**

**Scenario:** Reducing door-to-CT time for suspected stroke patients in the Emergency Department.

**1. Identify**

- People: ED physician, triage nurse, radiographer, radiologist, porter, stroke coordinator, bed manager
- Process: stroke activation, imaging order, patient transport, CT protocoling, scan completion, preliminary read
- Technology: CPOE, RIS, PACS, CT scanner, stroke alert messaging, network availability

**2. Question**
- **Success:** Eligible stroke patients reach CT within the target window, and images are available for review immediately after acquisition.
- **Assumptions:** Stroke alerts reach Radiology; CT capacity exists; transport is available; the stroke protocol is selected correctly.
- **Failure points:** Order delay, porter delay, occupied scanner, wrong CT protocol, PACS transmission delay.
- **Detection:** Door-to-order, order-to-scan, scan-to-image-available, rejected protocol counts, stroke huddle feedback.
- **Risk reduction:** Simulate the night shift, predefine scanner-priority rules, test alert routing, and rehearse protocol selection.
- **Response:** If the time target is missed, the stroke coordinator reviews the case the same day and escalates the blocking step to ED/Radiology leadership.

**3. Implement**
- Turn the answers into live controls: stroke pathway dashboard, daily exception review, named owners for each delay point, and escalation triggers.

**Subtext:** The loop forces the team to manage the full pathway, not just whether the CT scanner and PACS are technically working.

**Notes:** Keep this operational. This is about elapsed time, handoffs, and visibility. It should feel like a real hospital morning, not a framework demonstration.

------

### Slide 10 — Sample Scenario 2: Radiology Safety

**Title:** Critical Imaging Results, Run Through the Loop

**Content:**

**Scenario:** Ensuring critical radiology findings reach the responsible clinician and are acknowledged.

**1. Identify**
- People: radiologist, referring clinician, ward nurse, on-call team, radiology coordinator, IT support
- Process: report finalization, critical result flagging, clinician notification, acknowledgement, follow-up documentation
- Technology: RIS, PACS, EMR inbox, secure messaging, alert engine, audit log

**2. Question**
- **Success:** Critical findings are communicated, acknowledged, and documented within the required time window.
- **Assumptions:** The responsible clinician is correctly identified; alerts reach the right device; acknowledgment is captured; escalation rules are understood.
- **Failure points:** Wrong recipient, alert fatigue, unacknowledged messages, after-hours coverage gaps, result filed without clinical action.
- **Detection:** Unacknowledged critical alerts, time-to-acknowledgment, failed message delivery, after-hours escalation volume, and audit-log review.
- **Risk reduction:** Validate provider mapping, test after-hours rosters, define escalation tiers, and train radiologists and clinical teams on the acknowledgment workflow.
- **Response:** If acknowledgement breaches the trigger, escalate to the on-call service, document the contact path, and review repeated failures with clinical governance.

**3. Implement**
- Turn the answers into live controls: critical result dashboard, daily breach review, roster validation, audit sampling, and governance escalation.

**Subtext:** In radiology, the failure is not always image acquisition. Sometimes the scan is perfect, the report is correct, and the patient is still unsafe because the result did not reach the right person in time.

**Notes:** This example makes the patient-safety logic explicit and sets up the later point that technical and clinical success are not the same.

------

### Slide 11 — The Framework Does Not End at Go-Live

**Title:** Go-Live Is When the Evidence Gets Better

**Content:**

- **What go-live proves:** the system can be launched
- **What it does not prove:** staff are using it correctly, delays have disappeared, alerts are reaching the right people, or workarounds have stopped
- **What must continue:** dashboard review, floor observation, stakeholder check-ins, issue triage, ownership handoff, and escalation for unresolved risks
- **The practical rule:** keep watching until the new workflow is stable and operations can run the controls without the project team

## SECTION 3 — TECHNOLOGY LAYER

*3 slides | ~3 minutes*

------

### Slide 12 — Technology: What We're Managing

**Title:** Technology — The Enablement System

**Content:**

- **What it is:** the most visible layer — the system with a name, a vendor, a launch date, and a price tag
- **The visibility trap:** the fact that you can point to the system does not mean the system is working
- **The real success test:** the right system, reliably accessible, easy enough to use that people choose to use it, measurably improving the work it was meant to support — and that remains true long after launch

**Notes:** The "you can point to it" framing cuts through the common assumption that a running system is successful.

------

### Slide 13 — The Go-Live Trap

**Title:** Organizations Declare Victory at Precisely the Wrong Moment

**Content:** Timeline visual:

- **Launch** → support withdrawn → small problems accumulate → workarounds normalize → quiet deterioration → **visible failure** (months later)

**Subtext:** Adoption is the real measure — not whether the system *can* be used, but whether it *is* being used as intended, consistently, across all staff and all conditions. A system that staff work around has failed, regardless of how well it was built.

**Notes:** This is the most important single point in the Technology section for a non-technical audience. The timeline visual makes the failure mechanism concrete.

------

### Slide 14 — Six Technology Failure Areas

**Title:** Where Technology Actually Fails

**Content:** Table — one row per failure area, ⚠ marked:

- **T1 · Wrong system selected** — The system chosen does not fit how staff actually work
- **T2 · Access and Infrastructure Failures** — Staff cannot reliably access the system where the work happens
- **T3 · Wrong Information, Real Consequences** — The system produces unreliable or incorrect information ← , the highest patient safety risk
- **T4 · Staff not using the system** — Staff avoid the system and find other ways to get the work done
- **T5 · System does not improve the work** — The system is running, but the expected improvements are not happening
- **T6 · Decline after launch** — The system works at launch and gradually deteriorates

**Subtext:** 4 of 6 can fail without any obvious warning sign. T3 — Wrong Information, Real Consequences — is the most dangerous: errors here can affect patient safety, are often invisible until someone acts on wrong information, and are frequently misdiagnosed as user error.

**Notes:** Call out T3 explicitly. It's the one failure area in the Technology Layer that connects directly to patient harm — and it's also one of the most silent. Close by noting that technology failures quickly become process failures: when staff cannot trust or use the system, they create parallel workarounds.

------

## SECTION 4 — PROCESS LAYER

*3 slides | ~3 minutes*

------

### Slide 15 — Process: What We're Managing

**Title:** Process — The Execution System

**Content:**

- **What it is:** the transition from how things are done today to how they need to be done tomorrow
- **The hidden complexity:** every clinical environment runs on two versions of every process simultaneously — the official one (what the policy says, what the flowchart shows) and the real one (what staff actually do under time pressure, with limited resources)
- **What success actually requires:** the new workflow executing correctly, by all staff — not just champions — in real conditions, without regression

**Notes:** The "two versions" framing is the most useful thing to land here. It explains why conference room planning exercises so consistently miss the point.

------

### Slide 16 — The Transition Period

**Title:** The Most Dangerous Operational State in Any Implementation

**Content:**

- **What it is:** the interval when old and new processes run simultaneously — some records in the system, some on paper, no one with a complete picture of any given patient
- **Why it is dangerous:** mistakes happen, information gets lost, care coordination breaks down — not because of technical failure, but because two versions of reality are running at once
- **The 30-day rule:** within approximately 30 days of launch, if both processes are still running, staff stop perceiving the situation as temporary. It becomes the new normal.
- **The critical distinction:** a controlled step backward is recoverable. A normalized transition period is not — it is a failed implementation wearing the clothes of a work in progress.

**Notes:** The 30-day window is specific, surprising, and actionable. This is the most important concept in the entire Process section. Spend time here.

------

### Slide 17 — Seven Process Failure Areas

**Title:** Where Process Actually Fails

**Content:** Table — one row per failure area, ⚠ marked:

- **P1 · Getting the current picture wrong** — New process designed against a false picture of how things actually work today
- **P2 · Designed for Ideal Conditions** — Workflow works in planning, but falls apart in real operating conditions
- **P3 · Stuck Between Old and New** — Old and new processes run simultaneously until the hybrid becomes the new normal ← the organizing risk
- **P4 · Missing How Work Actually Gets Done** — Informal practices disrupted by the new process with no plan to replace them
- **P5 · Process working for champions, not for everyone** — Inconsistent execution hidden below headline compliance numbers
- **P6 · Process works but produces no improvement** — Compliance achieved; outcomes flat
- **P7 · Sliding back to the old way of working** — Future state erodes under pressure after the project closes

**Subtext:** 5 of 7 can fail without any obvious warning sign. P4 — Missing How Work Actually Gets Done — is the most reliably missed item in any planning exercise. Informal workarounds are not inefficiencies. They are compensating mechanisms for genuine gaps in the formal process. Removing them without replacement does not improve care — it exposes the gap they were covering.

**Notes:** The P4 note reframes workarounds for an audience that typically wants to eliminate them. Close by noting that many "technology problems" are process problems wearing a system label: the build reflects a hospital workflow that does not exist on the floor.

------

## SECTION 5 — PEOPLE LAYER

*3 slides | ~3 minutes*

------

### Slide 18 — People: What We're Managing

**Title:** People — The Behavior System

**Content:**

- **What it is:** where every person touched by the project stands in relation to the change, and whether they move in the direction the project needs
- **What makes it different from the other two layers:**
  - Technology failure is visible. A system goes down — someone notices, and an alert fires.
  - Process failure is observable. A workflow is not being followed — documentation gaps appear.
  - People's failure is neither. It accumulates in silence.
- **The longest tail:** Technology has a go-live date. Process has a future state target. People have neither.

**Notes:** The three-way contrast is the most important framing in this section. Let it land before moving on.

------

### Slide 19 — The Neutral Zone

**Title:** The Largest Addressable Population Is Waiting

**Content:**

- In nearly every Health IT implementation, the largest single population is neither actively resisting nor actively supporting — they are watching: the project's momentum, the behavior of people they respect, and the organizational signals around them.
- What moves them: not communication plans, not training programs, not executive mandates — but peer influence from someone they trust who has already decided.
- The implication: champion *placement* matters more than champion *headcount*. Having ten enthusiastic advocates in informatics does not cover the gap in clinical operations.

**Subtext:** Moving even a portion of the neutral zone toward passive support has more impact on project momentum than converting a single active resistor. This is the highest-leverage activity in the entire People Layer.

**Notes:** This reframe — placement over headcount — is the most actionable insight in the People section for a leader who owns a champion network.

------

### Slide 20 — Seven People Failure Areas

**Title:** Where People Layer Actually Fails

**Content:** Table — one row per failure area, ⚠ marked:

- **Pe1 · Flying Blind** — You don't know who matters, where they stand, or what authority they hold
- **Pe2 · Influential Opponent** — The person everyone follows is working against you
- **Pe3 · Owner in Name Only** — Someone holds the accountability but doesn't act on it ← , more dangerous than no owner at all
- **Pe4 · Not Enough Advocates** — The right advocates aren't in the right places
- **Pe5 · Too Many Fence-Sitters** — The majority are still waiting to see who wins
- **Pe6 · Resistance Mismanagement** — Pushback is being handled the wrong way
- **Pe7 · Stale Situation** — Your picture of the room is out of date

**Subtext:** 5 of 7 can fail without any obvious warning sign — the highest silent failure concentration across all three layers. Pe3 — Owner in Name Only — deserves special attention: a stakeholder who holds a title and does not exercise it is *more dangerous* than no owner at all, because it creates the appearance of accountability without the substance.

**Notes:** The Pe3 callout is counterintuitive and will register with anyone who has managed governance structures. Close by stating the larger consequence: a technically sound system deployed into passive resistance will fail, and a well-designed process owned by nominal champions will regress.

------

## SECTION 6 — SYNTHESIS

*2 slides | ~2 minutes*

------

### Slide 21 — What To Do Next

**Title:** Take This Back to Your Current Project

**Content:**

**1. Pick one live project**
- Choose the one where failure would hurt patients, staff trust, or operational continuity.

**2. Locate the immediate risk**

| If your project is... | Watch for... |
| --- | --- |
| Starting out | False current state |
| Designing / building | Process decisions hidden in configuration |
| Approaching go-live | Unresolved assumptions |
| Just live | No one watching the evidence |
| Post-launch | No clear operational owner |

**3. Run the loop**
- Map the people, the real workflow, and the technology chain.
- Run the six questions, especially Question 4: *How will we know something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?*

**4. Assign the controls**
- Owner, cadence, dashboard or observation method, escalation trigger, and post-go-live handoff.

**Subtext:** The takeaway is not a new document. It is a change in operating discipline: make failure visible early, assign someone to watch it, and keep watching after launch.

**Notes:** This should be designed as a four-part action slide. The phase/risk table is the visual center; avoid making it look like a project lifecycle model.

------

### Slide 22 — Closing

**Title:** Failure Is the Default. Success Is Engineered.

**Content:**

> *"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology, from first decision to sustained adoption, moving the organization from current state to future state."*

**Subtext:** The framework does not promise a successful implementation. It gives you the discipline to detect, own, and act on failure before the organization gets stuck between current state and future state.

**Notes:** Return to the Core Statement. Close on the premise, not on optimism. The repetition is intentional — the audience first encountered this sentence on Slide 5 as a definition. They should hear it now as the operating standard for whether the change has actually landed. Questions welcome.
