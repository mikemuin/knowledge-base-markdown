Let's build the **Technology Failure Control Map** now. We have everything we need — the success definition gives us the failure domains directly by inversion, and the framework logic gives us the structure.A few things worth calling out as you review the map.

**The silent failure risk pattern.** Four of the six domains carry that marker — Selection, Integration, Adoption, and Strategic Misalignment. These are the domains where the system can appear healthy while actively failing. This is why your detection mechanisms matter as much as your prevention mechanisms. You can prevent a noisy failure with good planning. Silent failure requires deliberate instrumentation.

**Domain 3 is your highest technical risk in Health IT.** Integration and data quality failure is marked danger deliberately. It is the domain most likely to produce a patient safety event, most likely to be invisible until a clinician acts on bad data, and most likely to have its root cause misattributed — teams fix the symptom (a wrong result) without finding the actual break (a semantic mapping error introduced six months prior). It deserves the most rigorous prevention and detection investment of all six.

**Domain 4 is the bridge.** Notice the owner note on Adoption — it explicitly crosses into the People layer. This is by design. A system that is technically flawless but behaviorally ignored has failed. Your Technology control map ends at the People layer's edge. When we build the People map, Domain 4 will have a mirror on that side.

**Post-live degradation is the most consistently underfunded.** Organizations treat go-live as the finish line. Sustainment is the afterthought. But this is where the compounding begins — a small knowledge gap becomes a large one, a minor technical debt becomes an unmaintainable system, and a vendor relationship left cold becomes a support nightmare. The prevention actions here are almost entirely organizational, not technical.
