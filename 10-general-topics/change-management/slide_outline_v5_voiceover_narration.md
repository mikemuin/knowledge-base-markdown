# Voice-Over Narration Script

## Failure-Aware Change Management Framework

**Format:** Video lecture voice-over **Slides:** 22 **Estimated runtime:** 17-20 minutes **Version:** 5

------

## SECTION 1 — INTRODUCTION

------

### Slide 1 — Title

*Why Health IT Projects Fail — And What To Do About It*

This framework comes from field experience: Health IT projects that succeeded, projects that failed, and the more dangerous category — projects that failed quietly enough that no one could agree they had failed at all.

This is not a theory of change management from a conference room. It is about what happens inside hospitals when systems, workflows, and clinical behavior must all change simultaneously.

------

### Slide 2 — The Problem

*The Pattern That Keeps Repeating*

Three statements recur after Health IT projects disappoint.

"The system went live on time, but staff never really adopted it."

"The process looked right on paper, but that is not how anyone actually worked."

"Leadership was supportive, until they weren't."

These sound like three different failures. They are not. They are the same failure wearing different clothes.

The pattern repeats because most change management approaches are built to manage success. They are less adept at managing the failure already forming beneath the surface.

------

### Slide 3 — The Central Premise

*This Framework Starts From the Opposite Assumption*

Most change frameworks are optimistic by design. They assume that if the right steps are followed, adoption will happen. Resistance is treated as the exception.

This framework starts somewhere less comfortable.

Failure is the default. Success is engineered.

Not because the people are incompetent. Not because the technology is bad. Not because the organization does not care. Failure is the default because clinical environments are complex, busy, political, and unforgiving.

Engineered does not mean over-planned. It means detected, owned, prevented where possible, and contained when prevention fails.

------

### Slide 4 — The Silent Failure Problem

*The Failures That Make No Noise*

In most projects, failure announces itself. A missed deadline shows up on the plan. A budget overrun is reported. A system outage fires an alert.

In Health IT change management, the most consequential failures often make no noise.

A stakeholder who was supportive in month one drifts into disengagement by month four. No alert fires.

A clinical champion starts to withdraw. No report captures it.

Data begins flowing incorrectly between two systems that both appear to be working. No dashboard turns red.

In each case, the project looks fine from the outside. The problem is accumulating underneath.

This is why detection cannot be passive. It has to be actively designed — with instruments, cadences, and named owners whose specific job is to look for things that are not announcing themselves.

The question most teams skip: "How will we know something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?"

It gets skipped because it is uncomfortable. It requires looking for evidence that the project is not working, at the exact moment when the organization wants proof that it is.

------

### Slide 5 — The Framework at a Glance

*The Core Statement*

The framework starts with one sentence: the Core Statement.

*"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

There are two phrases to hold onto.

"Concurrent with project execution" means change management starts on day one, not after go-live.

"Integrity of the transition" means the real goal is not launch. The real goal is a stable operating state after the launch.

This is not a softer project-management slogan. It is an operating definition.

The work is to manage failure while execution is happening, because in Health IT the transition itself is where clinical continuity, trust, and data integrity are most exposed.

------

### Slide 6 — The Framework at a Glance

*Governing Principles. Three Layers.*

From that Core Statement come five Governing Principles.

First: **Start on day one, not day done.** If change management starts after configuration, the most important decisions have already been made.

Second: **Engineer for failure, not optimism.** Assume the plan will break somewhere, then design the controls before it does. Failure is the default; success is engineered through detection, ownership, prevention, and response.

Third: **What you can't see will hurt you most.** Silent failure needs active detection, not passive reporting.

Fourth: **If everyone owns it, no one owns it.** Accountability for the transition has to be assigned. It will not appear by goodwill.

Fifth: **Not all failures cost the same.** A training gap is recoverable. A patient safety event is not. The response has to match the consequence.

The framework then works across three layers: People, Process, and Technology. These are not silos — they are three surfaces of the same transition.

------

## SECTION 2 — HOW TO USE THE FRAMEWORK

------

### Slide 7 — The Three-Step Loop

*Identify. Question. Implement.*

The framework is used in a simple loop: identify, question, implement.

**Identify** — means taking inventory. Who is involved? What workflows are changing? Which systems, data flows, and infrastructure are in scope?

**Question** — means running the same six questions against that inventory.

**Implement** — means turning the answers into live project controls: checkpoints, trackers, detection schedules, prevention tasks, and response protocols.

In practice, this means success definitions become gate criteria, assumptions go into a live tracker, risks go into a register, detection runs on a schedule, prevention becomes assigned project work, and responses are staged before the incident.

The important point is that this is not a workshop exercise. It repeats at every major milestone, through go-live, and into sustainment.

------

### Slide 8 — The Six Questions

*The Same Six Questions. Applied to Everything.*

You do not need to memorize the six questions. Listen for the pattern: define success, test assumptions, name failure points, design detection, reduce risk early, and prepare the response.

First: **What does success look like?** It has to be specific enough that two people can agree whether it happened.

Second: **What do we think is true?** Every unconfirmed assumption is a risk wearing a nicer name.

Third: **Where could we fail?** Sort the answers by consequence: low, high, and irreversible.

Fourth: **How will we know if something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?** This is the question most teams skip. It requires data, observation, conversations, behavioral signals, a cadence, and a named owner.

Fifth: **What do we do now to reduce the chance of failure?** Prevention without an owner and a deadline is only an intention.

Sixth: **If something fails, what do we do?** The response needs a trigger, steps, an owner, and a recovery target. For patient safety risks, the first step is containment. Stop the harm first. Understand it second.

------

### Slide 9 — Sample Scenario 1: Radiology Access

*ED Stroke CT, Run Through the Loop*

Let us make the loop concrete with Radiology.

First scenario: the hospital wants to reduce door-to-CT time for suspected stroke patients in the Emergency Department.

First, **Identify**.

The People Layer includes the ED physician, triage nurse, radiographer, radiologist, porter, stroke coordinator, and bed manager. If one of those roles is invisible in the plan, the pathway will find that gap at the worst possible time.

The Process Layer is the actual stroke pathway: activation, imaging order, patient transport, CT protocoling, scan completion, and preliminary read.

The Technology Layer is CPOE, RIS, PACS, the CT scanner, stroke alert messaging, and network availability. In Radiology, the technology chain is only as strong as the handoff between systems.

Second, **Question**.

Success is not "the CT system is available." Success is eligible stroke patients reaching CT within the target window, with images available for review immediately after acquisition.

The assumptions are practical: stroke alerts reach Radiology, CT capacity exists, transport is available, and the stroke protocol is selected correctly.

The failure points are where the clock gets lost: order delay, porter delay, occupied scanner, wrong CT protocol, or PACS transmission delay.

Detection must follow the pathway. Track door-to-order, order-to-scan, and scan-to-image-available. Review rejected protocol counts. Listen to stroke huddle feedback. If the data says the scanner is available but patients are still late to CT, the problem is probably not the scanner.

Risk reduction then becomes real project work: simulate the night shift, predefine scanner priority rules, test alert routing, and rehearse protocol selection before go-live.

And the response is specific. If the time target is missed, the stroke coordinator reviews the case the same day and escalates the blocking step to ED and Radiology leadership.

Third, **Implement**.

The answers become live controls: a stroke pathway dashboard, daily exception review, named owners for each delay point, and escalation triggers.

That is the loop. It turns a broad ambition, "reduce stroke imaging delay," into visible control points that can actually be managed.

------

### Slide 10 — Sample Scenario 2: Radiology Safety

*Critical Imaging Results, Run Through the Loop*

Second scenario: critical radiology results.

This is where Health IT teams can fool themselves. The scan may be completed. The report may be correct. The message may even be sent. The patient is still unsafe if the responsible clinician never receives it, never acknowledges it, or no one acts on it.

First, **Identify**.

The People Layer includes the radiologist, referring clinician, ward nurse, on-call team, radiology coordinator, and IT support.

The Process Layer includes report finalization, critical result flagging, clinician notification, acknowledgement, and follow-up documentation.

The Technology Layer includes RIS, PACS, the EMR inbox, secure messaging, the alert engine, and the audit log.

Second, **Question**.

Success means critical findings are communicated, acknowledged, and documented within the required time window.

The assumptions are where many implementations are dangerously optimistic: the responsible clinician is correctly identified, alerts reach the right device, acknowledgement is captured, and escalation rules are understood.

The failure points are predictable: wrong recipient, alert fatigue, unacknowledged messages, after-hours coverage gaps, or a result filed without clinical action.

Detection has to be active. Monitor unacknowledged critical alerts, time-to-acknowledgement, failed message delivery, after-hours escalation volume, and audit-log samples.

Risk reduction means validating provider mapping, testing after-hours rosters, defining escalation tiers, and training both radiologists and clinical teams on the acknowledgement workflow.

The response is also defined in advance. If acknowledgement breaches the trigger, escalate to the on-call service, document the contact path, and review repeated failures with clinical governance.

Third, **Implement**.

The answers become a critical result dashboard, daily breach review, roster validation, audit sampling, and governance escalation.

This is the key point: in Radiology, technical success and clinical success are not the same thing. A beautiful PACS implementation that fails to close the communication loop is still a patient safety problem.

------

### Slide 11 — The Framework Does Not End at Go-Live

*Go-Live Is When the Evidence Gets Better*

Now we can say the go-live point plainly.

Go-live proves that the system can be launched. It does not prove that staff are using it correctly, that delays have disappeared, that alerts are reaching the right people, or that workarounds have stopped.

In hospital operations, the first honest evidence often appears after launch. That is when the night shift uses the system. That is when the scanner is occupied, the roster is wrong, the alert goes to the wrong clinician, or the team finds a shortcut no one saw in testing.

So the framework does not stop at go-live.

The dashboard review continues. Floor observation continues. Stakeholder check-ins continue. Issue triage continues. Ownership is handed to operations, but the controls are not abandoned at the handoff.

The practical rule is simple: keep watching until the new workflow is stable, the old workarounds have stopped, and operations can run the controls without the project team holding them up.

## SECTION 3 — TECHNOLOGY LAYER

------

### Slide 12 — Technology: What We're Managing

*Technology — The Enablement System*

The Technology Layer is the most visible layer. It has a vendor, a name, a launch date, and a price tag.

In the Technology Layer, engineered success means adoption is designed, measured, and sustained. It is not inferred from go-live.

That visibility creates overconfidence.

A system that exists is not the same as a system that works. A system that is running is not the same as one that is used correctly, consistently, and safely.

Technology success means the right system is in place, reliably accessible, easy enough that staff choose to use it, and measurably improves the work it was meant to support.

And that has to remain true after launch day.

------

### Slide 13 — The Go-Live Trap

*Organizations Declare Victory at Precisely the Wrong Moment*

The most dangerous assumption in Health IT is that going live means the technology has succeeded.

After launch, support begins to withdraw. Small issues accumulate. Staff find workarounds for friction points. Those workarounds become habits.

Months later, what looks like a technology failure has often been building since the week after launch.

The real measure is adoption. Not whether the system can be used, but whether it is being used as intended, by the right staff, across real conditions.

A system that staff work around has failed, no matter how smoothly it launched.

------

### Slide 14 — Six Technology Failure Areas

*Where Technology Actually Fails*

There are six technology failure areas. Do not try to memorize the list. Listen for the pattern: the system can be present and available yet still clinically unreliable.

First: **wrong system selected.** The system does not fit how staff actually work.

Second: **access and infrastructure failures.** Staff cannot reliably reach the system where the work happens.

Third: **wrong information, real consequences.** This is the highest-risk area. Incorrect data can directly affect patient safety, and the failure may be invisible until someone acts on the wrong information.

In Health IT, the dangerous failure is not always an interface going down; it is an allergy, medication, result, diagnosis, or patient identifier moving across systems in a way that is technically accepted and clinically wrong.

Fourth: **staff not using the system.** The system is available, but too slow, too cumbersome, or too disconnected from real workflows.

Fifth: **the system does not improve the work.** It runs, but the promised improvement never appears.

Sixth: **decline after launch.** The system works at launch, then deteriorates as support withdraws and small problems compound.

Four of these six can fail silently. That is why technology monitoring cannot stop at uptime.

And once staff stop trusting the system, the failure does not stay technical. They create paper lists, parallel spreadsheets, informal notes, and unit-specific workarounds. Within weeks, the hospital is no longer running one process. It is running several.

So if a technology problem keeps returning, do not assume the root cause is technical. It may be sitting in the workflow, the governance, or the people who were never brought into the decision early enough.

------

## SECTION 4 — PROCESS LAYER

------

### Slide 15 — Process: What We're Managing

*Process — The Execution System*

The Process Layer manages the transition from how work is done today to how it needs to be done after the change.

In the Process Layer, engineered success means the handover from the old way to the new way is actively managed and completed — not left to settle on its own.

Every clinical environment has two versions of every process.

There is the official version: the policy, the flowchart, the version that leadership believes is happening.

Then there is the real version: what staff actually do under time pressure, during short-staffed shifts, and in the gaps that the formal process never solves.

The gap between those two versions is where implementations fall apart.

Process success is not a signed-off workflow. It is the future-state workflow being executed correctly by actual clinical staff under real-world conditions, with workarounds addressed, improvements measured, and no quiet regression.

------

### Slide 16 — The Transition Period

*The Most Dangerous Operational State in Any Implementation*

Hold onto this phrase: transition period.

This is the interval during which old and new processes run simultaneously.

Some records are in the new system. Some are on paper. Some staff follow the new process. Some follow the old one. No one has a complete picture of any given patient.

This is where information gets lost, handoffs fail, and errors occur. Not because the system crashed, but because two versions of reality are running at once.

Within about thirty days, if both processes are still running side by side, staff stop treating the situation as temporary. It becomes normal.

Closing the transition requires more than training. It requires cutover rules, floor support, operational authority, and leadership attention that does not disappear after launch week.

A controlled step backward is recoverable. A normalized transition period is not. It is a failed implementation dressed as a work in progress.

------

### Slide 17 — Seven Process Failure Areas

*Where Process Actually Fails*

There are seven process failure areas. The pattern is simple: the process can look clean in governance and still fail on the floor.

First: **getting the current picture wrong.** The new process is designed against the official story, not the real work.

Second: **designed for ideal conditions.** It works well in planning but fails under short staffing, high demand, or system slowdown.

Third: **stuck between old and new.** The transition never closes. Old and new processes run side by side until the hybrid becomes the new normal.

Fourth: **missing how work actually gets done.** The informal practices staff rely on were never captured. The new process disrupts them with no plan to replace them.

Fifth: **the process works for champions, but not for everyone.** Aggregate compliance hides the weak unit, the night shift, or the reluctant department.

Sixth: **the process works but produces no improvement.** Compliance is achieved, but outcomes stay flat.

Seventh: **sliding back to the old way of working.** The future state erodes after the project team leaves.

Five of these seven can fail silently. Floor reality has to be checked directly. The spreadsheet will not tell the whole truth.

And when process is wrong, it is often misdiagnosed. A system built from meeting-room workflows will fail on the floor, and everyone will call it a technology problem. It is not. The technology is faithfully executing a process design that was never true.

This is why governance matters. Getting the current picture right requires front-line access. Closing the transition requires operational authority. If the right people cannot be activated at the right moment, the plan is paperwork.

------

## SECTION 5 — PEOPLE LAYER

------

### Slide 18 — People: What We're Managing

*People — The Behavior System*

Technology and process can look stable on a dashboard. People cannot.

In the People Layer, engineered success means stakeholder movement is tracked and managed. It is not left to communication plans and hope.

The People Layer manages where each person stands in relation to the change, and whether they are moving in the direction the project needs.

Technology failure is visible. A system goes down. Someone notices.

Process failure is observable. A handoff is missed. Documentation is split. Evidence appears.

People failure is neither. A stakeholder drifts from support to disengagement. A champion burns out. A senior leader approves in governance and slow-rolls in operations.

Even with formal measures, that leader may still appear supportive. Operationally, they are an obstacle.

And unlike the other layers, People has no finish line. Behavior does not resolve on a project timeline.

------

### Slide 19 — The Neutral Zone

*The Largest Addressable Population Is Waiting*

In most Health IT implementations, the largest group is neither actively resisting nor actively supporting.

They are watching.

They watch project momentum. They watch the behavior of people they respect. They watch whether leadership is serious after the first pressure point.

Moving even a part of this neutral zone toward passive support matters more than converting a single active resistor.

What moves them is usually not another email, another training module, or another executive message.

They are asking a social question: who has already decided, and do I trust that person's judgment?

That is why champion placement matters more than champion headcount. Ten enthusiastic advocates in informatics do not cover a gap in clinical operations.

------

### Slide 20 — Seven People Failure Areas

*Where People Layer Actually Fails*

There are seven People failure areas. The pattern is the same: none of them announces themselves.

First: **flying blind.** You don't know who matters, where they stand, or what authority they hold. You are making decisions without a map.

Second: **influential opponent.** The person everyone follows — the one whose opinion moves the room — is working against you.

Third: **owner in name only.** Someone holds the accountability but doesn't act on it. This is worse than having no owner, because it creates the appearance of control without the substance.

Fourth: **not enough advocates.** The right people aren't in the right places. Having ten enthusiastic supporters in one department does not cover the gap in another.

Fifth: **too many fence-sitters.** The majority are still waiting to see who wins. Communication has reached them. Their position hasn't moved.

Sixth: **resistance mismanagement.** Pushback is being handled the wrong way — either legitimate concerns are treated as opposition, or opposition is treated as a training problem.

Seventh: **stale situation.** Your picture of the room is out of date. The map was accurate at the start. The landscape changed, and no one updated it.

Five of these seven can fail silently. There is no dashboard for disengagement.

This is why People is not just another equal layer. It is what makes the other two work.

A technically sound system deployed into passive resistance will fail. A well-designed process owned by nominal champions will regress. Champions do not usually quit in a dramatic way. They fade, and by the time that is visible, both the process and the system have lost their day-to-day reinforcement.

People failure is usually the last to be noticed and the most expensive to recover.

------

## SECTION 6 — SYNTHESIS

------

### Slide 21 — What To Do Next

*Take This Back to Your Current Project*

So what do you do with this when you go back to your own project?

Start with one live project. Not the easiest one. Choose the one where failure would hurt patients, damage staff trust, disrupt operations, or create bad data that people will act on.

Then ask where that project is most exposed right now. On this slide, that is the table in the middle: project position on the left, immediate risk on the right.

If you are just starting out, the risk is a false current state. You may be documenting how people say work happens, not how it actually happens.

If you are designing and building, the risk is hidden process design. Every configuration decision is also a workflow decision, even when no one labels it that way.

If you are approaching go-live, the risk is unresolved assumptions. Everything you hope will sort itself out after launch is probably already a risk.

If you just went live, the risk is unattended evidence. This is when workarounds, delays, alert failures, and quiet resistance start becoming visible.

If you are post-launch, the risk is ownership drift. The project team has stepped back, but the receiving team may not actually be running the controls.

Then run the loop.

Map the three layers.

Who are the people who matter? Not just the steering committee. The people whose behavior will decide whether the change holds.

What is the real workflow? Not the policy version. The version that happens on the floor, on night shift, when the system is slow and the unit is short-staffed.

What is the technology chain? Not just the application name. The interfaces, devices, identifiers, reports, alerts, network points, and support paths that have to work together.

Then run the six questions. The most important one is still Question 4: how will we know something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?

Do not leave that as a discussion point. Turn it into controls.

Name the owner. Set the cadence. Choose the signal: dashboard, observation, audit sample, huddle, ticket review, stakeholder check-in. Define the trigger for escalation. Decide who receives ownership after go-live.

That is the practical takeaway: find the current risk, run the loop against it, and assign the controls.

That is what engineering success looks like in practice. Not a prettier plan. Not another governance ritual. A project that can see failure early enough to act before patients, staff, or operations absorb the cost.

------

### Slide 22 — Closing

*Failure Is the Default. Success Is Engineered.*

That brings us back to the Core Statement.

*"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

The first time, that sentence was a premise. Now it is a summary.

This framework does not promise a successful implementation. It gives you the structure to make failure visible early enough to act.

Before the old way and the new way are both still running, and no one can agree which one is real.

Before the advocates who made it work burn out and go quiet.

Before the person accountable for the outcome stops acting like they are.

Failure is the default. Success is engineered.

In Health IT, that discipline is not administrative overhead. It is how we protect clinical continuity, preserve trust, and keep bad data from becoming bad care.
