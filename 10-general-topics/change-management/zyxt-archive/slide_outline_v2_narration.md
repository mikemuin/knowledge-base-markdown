# Narration Script

## Failure-Aware Change Management Framework

**Format:** Video lecture **Slides:** 24 **Estimated runtime:** 18–20 minutes

------

## SECTION 1 — INTRODUCTION

------

### Slide 1 — Title

*Why Health IT Projects Fail — And What To Do About It*

This framework came out of field experience — from watching Health IT projects succeed, fail, and fail quietly enough that no one could agree they had failed at all. What follows is grounded in what actually happens inside hospitals during a digital transformation, not what the project plan says should happen.

------

### Slide 2 — The Problem

*The Pattern That Keeps Repeating*

Three statements come up in nearly every post-implementation review of a Health IT project that did not go as planned.

"The system went live on time — but staff never really adopted it."

"The process looked right on paper — but that is not how anyone actually worked."

"Leadership was supportive — until they weren't."

These feel like three different problems. They are not. They are the same failure, wearing different clothes. And the reason it keeps repeating is that most change management approaches are built to manage success — not to manage the failure that is already on its way.

------

### Slide 3 — The Central Premise

*This Framework Starts From the Opposite Assumption*

Most change management frameworks are optimistic by design. They assume that if the right steps are followed, adoption will happen. Resistance is treated as the exception — something to be managed when it shows up, not something to be expected from the start.

This framework starts from the opposite assumption.

Failure is the default. Not because the people involved are incompetent, or because the technology is bad, or because the organization does not care. But because change in a complex clinical environment is genuinely hard, and the forces working against it are real, persistent, and often invisible.

Success is not what happens when nothing goes wrong. It is what gets built deliberately against the forces that are always working to undo it.

------

### Slide 4 — The Silent Failure Problem

*14 of 20 Ways This Can Fail Generate No Alert*

In most project environments, failure announces itself. A missed deadline appears on the project plan. A budget overrun shows up in the financial report. A system outage fires an alert.

In Health IT change management, the most consequential failures do not announce themselves. A stakeholder who was genuinely supportive at the start drifts toward disengagement by month four. No alert fires. A clinical champion starts to quietly withdraw. No report captures it. Data begins flowing incorrectly between two systems that both appear to be working. No warning appears on any dashboard.

This framework identifies twenty distinct ways a Health IT implementation can fail. Fourteen of those twenty carry a high risk of silent failure — they can go seriously wrong without generating any obvious warning signal.

That is why detection — the active, deliberate search for early failure signals — is treated here as just as important as prevention. The question most project teams skip is not "where could we fail?" It is "how will we know if something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?" It gets skipped because it requires actively looking for evidence that the project is not working, at the exact moment when organizational pressure demands proof that it is.

------

### Slide 5 — The Framework at a Glance

*One Core Statement. Five Governing Principles. Three Layers.*

The entire framework derives from a single sentence — the Core Statement:

*"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

Two things in that sentence are worth holding. "Concurrent with project execution" means this work begins on day one — not after the system is live. And "the integrity of the transition" means the goal is not just a successful launch — it is a stable, sustained change in how the organization operates.

From the Core Statement, five Governing Principles are derived. The framework then operates across three layers — People, Process, and Technology — the three surfaces on which any Health IT implementation succeeds or fails.

------

## SECTION 2 — HOW TO USE THE FRAMEWORK

------

### Slide 6 — The Three-Step Loop

*Identify. Question. Implement.*

The framework is applied in three steps that repeat continuously — not as a phase that gets completed and closed, but as an ongoing management discipline.

**Identify** means taking inventory of what belongs to each layer. For People: who is involved, where they stand, who holds authority, and where resistance is coming from. For Process: how work is actually done today and what it needs to look like after the change. For Technology: the systems, the connections between them, the infrastructure, the data, and who will support it after go-live.

**Question** means running the same six questions — covered on the next slide — against everything in that inventory.

**Implement** means putting the answers to work inside the live project: as checkpoints, trackers, detection schedules, prevention tasks, and response protocols. That is where the thinking becomes action.

The loop then repeats — at every major milestone, after go-live, and into sustainment — until accountability has been formally transferred to the people who will own it in operations.

------

### Slide 7 — The Six Questions

*The Same Six Questions. Applied to Everything.*

**What does success look like?** Specifically — clear enough that two people could agree on whether it has been achieved, measurable, and time-bound.

**What are we taking for granted?** Every project runs on assumptions that are never written down. This question forces them into the open and separates the confirmed ones from the unconfirmed — which are risks in disguise.

**Where could we fail?** Sorted by consequence: low, high, and irreversible. With explicit attention to anything that could fail silently.

**How will we know if something is going wrong before it becomes a crisis?** The most commonly skipped question. It requires designing detection — data to track, observations to make, conversations to hold, behavioral signals to watch for — each with a cadence and a named owner. Without both, detection is an intention, not a discipline.

**What do we do now to reduce the chance of failure?** Deliberate prevention work, taken before anything goes wrong. Every measure needs an owner and a completion date. A measure that is listed but not assigned is not prevention.

**If something fails, what do we do?** A response planned during a crisis is a reaction. A response planned before a crisis is a protocol. Each response needs a specific trigger, defined steps, a named owner, and a recovery target. For the highest-consequence risks — especially patient safety — the first step is always containment. Stop the harm first. Understand it second.

------

### Slide 8 — Who Does This Work

*Two Types of Ownership. Both Are Required.*

The framework distinguishes between two types of ownership. Without both, gaps develop that no amount of tactical skill can close.

**Methodology ownership** belongs to the Change Manager — designing the approach, running the diagnostic instruments, managing the detection cadence, identifying problems, and escalating them. What the Change Manager cannot do is close problems that require organizational authority. That is not a limitation of skill. A Change Manager cannot physically close a transition period, remove a nominal owner, or mandate a practice standard across a clinical department.

**Authority ownership** belongs to the Project Sponsor and executive leadership — acting on what the methodology surfaces: closing a transition, challenging a vendor, delaying a go-live, or replacing someone who holds a title without exercising it. The Project Sponsor does not manage the methodology. The Project Sponsor creates the conditions under which it can work.

A Change Manager without executive backing can identify a permanent transition period and have no power to close it. A Project Sponsor without a functioning methodology has no reliable signal that the transition period has become permanent. Both are required. Neither substitutes for the other.

------

### Slide 9 — The Framework Does Not End at Go-Live

*The Project Ends. The Framework Doesn't.*

Go-live ends the project structure. It does not end the work.

What ends: the implementation team, the launch milestones, the intensive pre-launch activity.

What continues: the detection cadence, assumption tracking, stakeholder monitoring, process compliance checks, and system health reviews — for a minimum of twelve months.

The framework ends when that transfer is complete and confirmed — when the people inheriting ownership have the tools, the knowledge, and the authority to keep running what they are receiving. Not when the transfer is announced. When it is verified.

------

## SECTION 3 — TECHNOLOGY LAYER

------

### Slide 10 — Technology: What We're Managing

*Technology — The Enablement System*

The Technology Layer is the most visible of the three layers. It has a name, a vendor, a launch date, and a price tag. That visibility is both its greatest strength and its most consistent source of overconfidence.

A system that exists and is running is not the same as a system that is working. Those are different conditions, and the gap between them is where most technology failures live.

What technology success actually requires: the right system for how this organization's staff actually work; reliably accessible at every site and on every device; easy enough that people choose to use it; measurably improving the work it was meant to support — and all of that remaining true in the months and years that follow, not just on launch day.

------

### Slide 11 — The Go-Live Trap

*Organisations Declare Victory at Precisely the Wrong Moment*

The most dangerous assumption in Health IT is that going live means the technology has succeeded.

After launch, support resources are progressively withdrawn. Small problems get logged but not urgently addressed. Staff develop workarounds for friction points. Those workarounds become habits. Months later, what appears as a technology failure has actually been accumulating since the week after launch.

The true measure is adoption — not whether the system can be used, but whether it is being used as intended, consistently, by all relevant staff, across all conditions. A system that staff work around has failed, regardless of how well it was built or how smoothly it launched.

------

### Slide 12 — Six Technology Failure Areas

*Where Technology Actually Fails*

**Wrong system selection** — the system chosen does not fit how staff actually work. Most commonly caused by cost-driven decisions, limited staff input, or evaluation in controlled conditions that do not reflect reality.

**Access and infrastructure** — staff cannot reliably reach the system where they work. Untested assumptions about connectivity, devices, and power supply are the most common cause.

**Data accuracy and system connections** — the system produces incorrect information, often without warning. This is the highest-risk area. When data flowing between systems is incomplete or incorrectly mapped, staff may act on information they believe is correct when it is not. The consequences can directly affect patient safety — and the failure is often invisible until it has already happened.

**Staff not using the system** — the system is available but too slow, too cumbersome, or too disconnected from real workflows. Staff quietly find other ways.

**System not improving the work** — the system is running and used, but the improvements it was meant to deliver are not happening. Either the underlying process was never redesigned, or success was never defined before launch.

**Decline after launch** — the system works at launch and gradually deteriorates as support withdraws, vendor updates cause regressions, and small problems compound.

Four of these six areas carry a high risk of silent failure.

------

### Slide 13 — Technology: The Cross-Layer Effect

*Technology Problems Don't Stay in Technology*

When data accuracy fails, staff cannot trust the system. They develop compensating habits — paper logs, manual cross-checks. Those habits spread unevenly, and the formal work process becomes inconsistent across the organization — not from a process decision, but from a technology failure.

When staff stop using the system, the work splits. Part runs through the system, part runs outside it. No one has a complete picture of any given situation.

When a system is selected without genuine staff input, the trust lost in that decision is lost early and durably. People who might have been supportive become resistant. Those who were uncertain become more so.

Some of the most significant risks in the Technology Layer are not technical in origin. Treating them as purely technical produces technical solutions that miss the actual cause.

------

## SECTION 4 — PROCESS LAYER

------

### Slide 14 — Process: What We're Managing

*Process — The Execution System*

The Process Layer manages the transition from how things are done today to how they need to be done after the change. That sounds straightforward. It is not.

Every clinical environment runs on two versions of every process simultaneously. The official version — what the policy says, what the flowchart shows, what leadership believes is happening. And the real version — what staff actually do, shift by shift, under time pressure, in the gaps between what the formal process provides and what the work actually requires. The real version is rarely written down.

The gap between these two versions is where implementations succeed or fall apart. In most projects, that gap is never honestly measured before the future state is designed. The future gets built on a misrepresented picture of the present.

------

### Slide 15 — The Transition Period

*The Most Dangerous Operational State in Any Implementation*

The transition period is the interval where old and new processes run simultaneously. It opens at go-live and must be closed as quickly as possible.

During it, some records are in the new system and some are on paper. Some staff follow the new process, some follow the old one. No one has a complete picture of any given patient. This is where information gets lost, handoffs fail, and errors occur — not from technical malfunction, but because two versions of reality are running at once.

Within approximately thirty days of launch, if both processes are still running side by side, staff stop perceiving the situation as temporary. It becomes the new normal — and a normalised transition period is not a phase. It is a failed implementation.

A controlled step backward to the old process, with a defined relaunch plan, is recoverable. A normalised transition period is not.

------

### Slide 16 — Seven Process Failure Areas

*Where Process Actually Fails*

**Getting the current picture wrong** — the new process is designed against how things are officially supposed to work, not how they actually do.

**Designing a process that cannot survive reality** — the workflow looks sound in planning but falls apart under real conditions: short-staffed shifts, high demand, and system slowdowns.

**The transition period becoming permanent** — the organising risk of the entire layer, covered on the previous slide.

**Missing the unofficial workarounds** — the failure area most reliably overlooked. Unofficial workarounds are not inefficiencies. They are compensating mechanisms covering genuine gaps in the formal process. Remove them without replacement and the gap is exposed, not fixed.

**Process working for champions but not for everyone** — compliance looks acceptable in aggregate while being deeply inconsistent at the unit level. The average hides where the actual risk lives.

**Process works but produces no improvement** — the workflow is followed correctly and the outcomes the project was meant to deliver are not happening. Either the underlying process was broken before the system was built on top of it, or success was never defined before launch.

**Sliding back to the old way of working** — the future state erodes after the project closes, as pressure, turnover, and absent monitoring allow old habits to return.

Five of these seven areas carry a high risk of silent failure.

------

### Slide 17 — Process: The Cross-Layer Effect

*Process Problems Don't Stay in Process*

When the current state picture is inaccurate, the system gets configured against a false foundation. At deployment, the mismatch presents as a technology problem — poor fit, unexpected friction, staff resistance to the system. It began as a process failure months earlier.

When the transition period normalises, data splits between the old process and the new system. Reports draw on incomplete information. The system is technically operational but clinically untrustworthy.

When process regression becomes visible, the people who most invested in making it succeed face a credibility problem with the colleagues they championed it to. Their willingness to keep advocating collapses. Process regression pulls the People Layer down with it.

------

## SECTION 5 — PEOPLE LAYER

------

### Slide 18 — People: What We're Managing

*People — The Behavior System*

The People Layer manages where every person touched by the project stands in relation to the change — and whether they move in the direction the project needs.

Technology failure is visible. A system goes down, a report produces wrong numbers. Someone notices, an alert fires.

Process failure is observable. A workflow is not being followed, a handoff is missed. Breakdowns leave evidence.

People failure is neither. It accumulates in silence. A stakeholder drifts from supportive to disengaged over four months and generates no alert. A champion burning out withdraws from advocacy before anyone notices. A senior leader who approves everything in governance and slow-rolls everything in practice is, by every formal measure, a supporter — and by every operational measure, an obstacle.

And unlike the other two layers, People has no finish line. Technology has a go-live date. Process has a future state target. People has neither. Behavioral commitment and champion engagement do not resolve on a project timeline.

------

### Slide 19 — The Neutral Zone

*The Largest Addressable Population Is Waiting*

In nearly every Health IT implementation, the largest population is neither actively resisting nor actively supporting. They are watching — the project's momentum, the behavior of people they respect, and the organizational signals around them.

Moving even a portion of this neutral zone toward passive support has more impact on project momentum than converting a single active resistor. The neutral zone is large. Active resistors are few.

What moves people in the neutral zone is not a communication plan, not an executive mandate, and not a training program. Those address knowledge and obligation. What the neutral zone is weighing is a social question — who has already decided, and do I trust that person's judgment?

The answer comes through peer influence — a colleague they trust who has already decided and is visibly living with it. This is the logic of the champion network, and why placement matters more than headcount. Coverage is a placement problem, not a counting problem.

------

### Slide 20 — Seven People Failure Areas

*Where People Actually Fails*

**Stakeholder mapping failure** — the project team does not have an accurate picture of where key people stand, why they are there, or what authority they hold.

**Critical node exposure** — someone who controls decisions, resources, information flow, or the informal network sits in resistance, with no active management of that gap.

**The authority-behavior gap** — a stakeholder holds formal accountability but does not exercise it. They approve in meetings and do not follow through in operations. This is more dangerous than having no owner at all — it creates the appearance of accountability without the substance.

**Champion deficit** — the advocate network does not cover the terrain it needs to cover, because champions are absent from critical areas, concentrated where support already exists, or burning out.

**Neutral zone inertia** — the undecided population is not moving, despite communication and training reaching them.

**Resistance mismanagement** — resistance is escalated when it should be engaged, or treated as uniform when it requires individual diagnosis. Addressing someone's legitimate concern as if it were political opposition often hardens their resistance.

**Tracking decay and regression** — the stakeholder map was accurate at initiation and was never updated. The landscape shifted. The map says the project is safe. It is not.

Five of these seven carry a high risk of silent failure — the highest concentration across all three layers.

------

### Slide 21 — People: The Cross-Layer Effect

*People Is Not the Third Equal. It Is the Substrate.*

When the authority-behavior gap is present, process decisions that require authority go unmade. Execution inconsistency goes unchallenged. Regression goes unaddressed. Every part of the process that depends on ownership becomes exposed the moment that ownership is nominal.

When the neutral zone is not moving, adoption fails regardless of how well the system was built. A technically sound system, properly configured, adequately trained — it will not reach consistent use if the behavioral conditions for adoption were never created.

When champions burn out, both the Process and Technology layers lose their primary sustaining mechanism simultaneously — without any obvious triggering event.

People is the behavioral substrate on which the other two layers operate. A technically sound system deployed into passive resistance will fail. A well-designed process owned by nominal champions will regress.

------

## SECTION 6 — SYNTHESIS

------

### Slide 22 — The Three Failure Chains

*How Failure Actually Moves Across the Framework*

Understanding individual failure areas is useful. Understanding how they chain together is what makes early intervention possible.

Three failure chains appear most consistently and carry the highest combined consequence.

The first is **The Silent Parallel State**.

It begins with an inaccurate picture of how work is actually done. The future state is designed against the sanitised version and cannot hold under real conditions. Staff find the new process unworkable and begin routing around it — which means routing around the system. The transition period never closes. Data splits between the formal system and the workarounds staff have developed. Reports become unreliable. The clinical staff who were sceptical from the start now have observable evidence that the system does not work. The resistance that was there from the beginning hardens, now with legitimate justification behind it.

The second is **The Post-Launch Collapse**.

It begins when support resources withdraw after go-live — the normal pattern. The system deteriorates gradually. The champions who staked their credibility on the project face a trust problem with their peers and disengage. The process they were sustaining begins to erode. No one is tracking any of it, because the stakeholder map was last updated at launch and the monitoring cadence was wound down when the project team left.

The third is **The Authority Vacuum**.

It begins when a senior leader holds formal accountability but does not exercise it. Process ownership becomes nominal. The system runs. The improvements do not materialise, because neither the process nor the governance was ever held to account for delivering them. Critical decisions get made by default rather than by authority. The gap between what the system was designed to do and what the organization needs widens — quietly — until it is no longer bridgeable.

Each chain has a specific entry point. Intervening there, early, is far less costly than managing the chain after it has run.

------

### Slide 23 — Implement: The Project Lifecycle

*The Framework Across the Project*

The framework does not change across project phases, but the risks that demand the most attention do.

In **Initiation and Discovery**, the primary risk is building on a false foundation. This is where the stakeholder map is established, real workflows are observed at actual sites, infrastructure is assessed, data quality is reviewed, and success criteria and baselines are defined — before any change is made.

In **Design and Configuration**, the primary risk is embedding failure into the design. Process decisions are being made inside system configuration, by technical staff, without anyone recognizing them as process decisions. By the time the system goes live, the process has already been decided.

In **Testing and Piloting**, the primary risk is a clean test environment that does not predict real deployment. This is also the last point at which response protocols can be reviewed and refined before the stakes become real.

In **Go-Live and Transition**, the primary risk is treating go-live as the finish line. Everything in this phase aims at closing the transition period quickly — with concentrated on-the-floor support, active leadership presence, unit-level monitoring, and a defined escalation trigger if the transition has not closed within thirty days.

In **Post-Live Sustainment**, the primary risk is failure that accumulates after the project team leaves. Formal ownership transfer, compliance monitoring for a minimum of twelve months, and scheduled stakeholder reassessments are not optional. They are the condition under which everything that came before them holds.

------

### Slide 24 — Closing

*Failure Is the Default. Success Is What You Build.*

At the start of this presentation, one sentence was introduced as the foundation of this framework. It is worth reading again now that the full structure behind it has been laid out.

*"Change management is the disciplined management of failure across People, Process, and Technology — concurrent with project execution — to protect the integrity of the transition from the current to the future state."*

The first time that sentence appears, it is a premise. The second time, it is a summary.

This framework does not promise a successful implementation. What it offers is the structure to make failure visible early enough to do something about it — before the silent parallel state has normalised, before the post-launch collapse has begun, before the authority vacuum has quietly swallowed the improvements the project was designed to deliver.

The work is the discipline. The discipline is the protection.
